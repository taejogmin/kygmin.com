## 0. The Introduction — Framing Only
**Exam importance: 🟢 Low (no direct questions), but it positions the reading**

- Output **fluctuates around its longer-term path**; this reading studies the **recurring, cyclical** fluctuations — the short-run counterpart to M3's growth material.
- **The overlap-and-split worth one line:** some short-term drivers (population, technology, capital) are the **same factors** behind long-term sustainable growth (🔗 M3 §12 LRAS shifters / §17). But **AD/AS shift forces — expectations, political developments, natural disasters, fiscal and monetary policy decisions — influence economies particularly in the short run.** This is Exhibit 16's split (cost/price vs. resource factors) restated as a time-horizon statement.
- **Definition to keep:** business cycles are **expansions and contractions in economic activity affecting broad segments of the economy** — the "broad segments" qualifier matters (a single-industry slump is not a business cycle).
- **Each cycle is different**, so analysts focus on the **expectations and decisions of businesses and households** → sector/company performance → **financial conditions and risk appetite** → portfolio exposures to **sectors and styles** (the investment angle; 🔗 M3 §14 is the mechanical version of this).

## 1. Consumer Behavior Through the Cycle
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — the four-phase model (Exhibit 1) and the durables/non-durables/services cyclicality ranking are the core testable grid of this reading so far**

### Why consumers come first 🟡
**Households are the largest single sector of most economies** → household consumption patterns determine overall economic direction **more than any other sector**. That makes them relevant to all practitioners, not just analysts covering consumer companies. (Scale check from 🔗 M3 Exhibit 7: C/GDP ≈ 50–70% across developed economies.)

### Consumer confidence 🟡
- Reflects **expectations of future incomes and employment prospects**.
- **Survey-based** → a *general guide to trends*, used to gauge how aggressive consumers may be in future spending, beyond direct observation of spending and its mix.
- 🔗 M3 §11 / Exhibit 14: consumer confidence ↑ → C ↑ → **AD shifts right**. This section is the measurement side of that shifter.

### The four-phase cycle — Exhibit 1 🔴 MEMORIZE THE GEOMETRY
The y-axis is the **GAP between actual GDP and trend GDP**. Phases are defined by **two coordinates: above/below trend AND rising/falling** — never by the direction of GDP alone.

```
gap
 +      ___Peak___
 |     /          \
 0 ---/------------\----------→ time
 |   /              \        /
 |  /                \      /
 | Trough             Trough
   |Recovery|Expansion|Slowdown|Contraction|
```

| Phase | Position vs. trend | Direction of the gap |
|---|---|---|
| **Recovery** | **Below** trend | **Rising** (from trough toward trend) |
| **Expansion** | **Above** trend (crosses the trend line) | **Rising** (to the peak) |
| **Slowdown** | **Above** trend | **Falling** (from peak back toward trend) |
| **Contraction** | **Below** trend | **Falling** (to the trough) |

⚠️ **The built-in traps:**
- "Growth is decelerating" ≠ contraction. A **slowdown** is still **above trend** — the economy is doing *better than average*, just decelerating. Contraction = below trend AND falling.
- A **recovery** is below trend — the economy is still doing *worse than average* even though it's improving. Level vs. direction, every time.
- Peak and trough are **turning points**, not phases: peak separates expansion from slowdown; trough separates contraction from recovery.

### The phase grid (Exhibit 1 table) 🔴 reproduce it

| | **Recovery** | **Expansion** | **Slowdown** | **Contraction** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Incomes, employment, confidence** | Unemployment **remains above average**; layoffs slow; businesses rely on **overtime before hiring**; confidence **starts improving** | **Hiring restarts**; unemployment **stabilizes and starts falling**; rising incomes, healthy employment prospects, greater confidence | Businesses **continue hiring but slower**; unemployment **continues to fall**; incomes **still growing**; consumers **remain confident** | Businesses **first cut overtime → freeze hiring → start layoffs**; employment declines; confidence weakens |
| **Durables** (autos, motorcycles, appliances, furniture) | Limited — households **postpone** | Increases | **Above average** | **Postponed; decreasing** |
| **Non-durables** (medicines, food, household products) | — little change — | — little change — | — little change — | — little change — |
| **Services** (entertainment, outdoor eating, communications, personal services) | Below average | Increases | Above average | Declines |

### The employment sequencing inside the grid ⚠️ favorite trap material
- **Entering a recovery:** firms use **overtime before new hiring** (hiring is a commitment) → unemployment stays **above average** well into the recovery.
- **Entering a contraction:** firms **cut overtime first, then freeze hiring, then lay off** → employment declines only after the downturn is underway.
- **In a slowdown:** unemployment is **still falling** and incomes **still growing** even as the economy decelerates.

> **One-liner:** *employment reacts late in both directions* — the labor market is effectively a **lagging** signal of the cycle. The stem will describe "unemployment still falling" and tempt you toward "expansion"; check whether growth is decelerating (→ slowdown).

### Cyclicality ranking 🔴 the cleanest exam point here
```
Business investment (I)  >  Durables  >  Services  >  Non-durables
   most cyclical                                        least cyclical
```
- **Durables = most cyclical** consumer category; **non-durables = least** (food and medicine get bought in any phase); **services in aggregate sit between** (they mix more- and less-cyclical sub-components).
- **Even durables are less cyclical than business investment** — 🔗 M3 §6: I (especially inventories) is the most volatile GDP component.
- **Durables as an early signal:** purchases replace items with longer useful lives, so they're **postponable** → **weakness in durables spending = early indication of general economic weakness; a pickup = signal of a broader cyclical recovery.** (Same postponement logic as 🔗 M1 §4's durable-goods elasticity exception — one mechanism, two readings.)

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED EXAMPLE 1, Q1 HERE
> *"Durable goods have the most pronounced cyclical behavior because:"* — you picked **C (needed more than non-durables/services)**; correct is **A (longer useful life)**.
>
> **The mechanism is postponability, and it runs through useful life:** durables are big-ticket items whose life can be **extended with repairs** without high replacement cost → consumers **delay replacement** when the outlook is poor → demand collapses in downturns and rebounds in recoveries. That *is* the cyclicality.
>
> **Why C is backwards:** if durables were "needed more," households would buy them in every phase — that would make them **LESS** cyclical, like food and medicine. Necessity → stable demand → low cyclicality. The non-durables row of the grid ("little change through the cycle") is the proof.
> **Why B is backwards:** "cannot be delayed" is the exact opposite of the mechanism.
>
> **Fix — one causal chain to memorize:** `long useful life → replacement is deferrable → purchases postponed in downturns → most cyclical`. When a "because" question offers a reason, test it: *would this reason make demand MORE stable or LESS stable?* Only "less stable" explains cyclicality.

### Measures of consumption 🟡
Two primary measures:
1. **Retail sales** (physical and online)
2. **Broad-based indicators of consumer spending** — include purchases *outside* purely retail establishments (utilities, household services)

Finer measures track spending on specific product groups, **real and nominal** (🔗 M3 §5: real is the activity measure).

### Income growth and permanent income 🔴 definition trap
- Income growth = good indicator of consumption prospects; **less timely than consumer surveys** but widely available.
- Most relevant: **after-tax income = disposable income** (🔗 M3 §8: C = C(Y − T)).
- **Permanent income** = **excludes temporary income** (windfalls, sudden/unexpected items) **and unsustainable gains/losses** — the income flow households **believe they can rely on**.

**The three-way mapping — what each income concept predicts 🔴**

| Behavior | Best predicted by | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic / discretionary consumption — incl. services** | **Permanent income** | Households set their ongoing consumption level on the income they believe they can rely on, not on temporary fluctuations |
| **Durables spending** | **Disposable income, regardless of source** — windfalls included | A windfall doesn't change grocery or dining habits, but it does buy the car you'd been postponing |
| **Saving rate** | **Short-term uncertainty** (precautionary motive) | Reflects perceived risk to future income during recessions — NOT the permanent income level |

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED EXAMPLE 1, Q2 HERE
> *"Permanent income provides a better guide to:"* — you picked **A (saving rates)**; correct is **B (spending on services)**.
>
> **Why B:** services (entertainment, dining out, communications) are **discretionary**, and households adjust discretionary consumption to the **perceived permanent income level**, not to temporary earnings swings. Services are the "basic level of consumption" bucket in the curriculum's logic.
> **Why A is wrong:** the saving rate is driven by **short-term uncertainty caused by recessions** — the precautionary motive from the saving-rates section. It's a fear gauge, not a permanent-income gauge.
> **Why C is wrong:** durables follow **disposable income regardless of source** — the opposite of the permanent-income concept.
>
> **The trap you fell into:** the curriculum's "basic level of consumption reflects permanent income" never names services explicitly, so the link had to be inferred: services are discretionary → discretionary spending is set by reliable income → permanent income. The three-way table above is the fix — **permanent income ↔ services; disposable income ↔ durables; uncertainty ↔ saving rate.** Three behaviors, three different drivers — the exam will pair them up wrong.

### Saving rates — the two-sided signal 🔴
- Spending frequently **diverges from income trends**, however income is measured → watch the **saving rate** (usually % of income).
- A change in the saving rate captures **intent to reduce spending out of current income**; it also reflects **perceived future income uncertainty → precautionary saving**.

| Signal | Reading |
|---|---|
| **Saving rate RISES** | Caution among households → **signals economic weakening** (near-term negative) |
| **Large accumulated STOCK of savings** + wide income–spending gap | **Greater capacity to increase spending** → longer-term **recovery potential** |

> **One-liner:** *unusually high savings say something negative about the cycle at first, but point longer-term to the potential for recovery.* ⚠️ Flow (rate rising = caution now) vs. stock (savings pile = future firepower) — distractors present only one side. (Also fits 🔗 M3 Exhibit 12: UK saving rate spiked in the 2009–10 downturn while house prices stalled.)

## 2. Housing Sector Behavior (§4.03)
**Exam importance: 🟡→🔴 Medium-High — short section, but it carries two guaranteed-style items: "small sector, outsized cyclical impact" and the credit-cycle sequencing (sales → inventory → construction)**

### Why housing matters more than its size 🔴
- Housing activity = **new and existing home sales + residential construction**.
- **Small part of the economy** relative to consumer spending — **but experiences dramatic swings**, so it **counts more in overall economic movements than its size suggests**. (Same shape as business investment in 🔗 M3 §6: small share, high volatility, big cyclical footprint.)
- In the US and UK, **property values link closely to consumer wealth and confidence** → 🔗 M3 §11: housing prices ↑ → wealth effect → C ↑ → AD right. Housing is a *transmission channel* into the consumer sector, not just a sector of its own.

### Available statistics 🟢
- **Inventory of unsold homes** on the market
- **Average or median home price** — by housing-unit type, or per square foot/meter
- These relationships "follow fairly regular cyclical patterns."

### Interest-rate sensitivity 🔴 the direction call
Most buyers finance with a **mortgage** → sector is **especially sensitive to interest rates**:
```
Loan rates ↓ → home buying ↑ → construction ↑
Loan rates ↑ → home buying ↓ → construction ↓
```
(🔗 M3 §9 interest-rate effect, housing-specific: residential real estate is the textbook loan-financed purchase.)

### Housing's own internal cycle — part of the CREDIT cycle 🔴
Beyond rates, housing runs an internal cycle driven by **affordability**:

**Affordability measure:** compare **household income** with the **cost of supporting an average house** = its **price + the expense of a typical mortgage**. Low prices relative to incomes + low mortgage rates → cost of owning ↓ → demand ↑.

**The maturing-expansion sequence — memorize the order:**
```
Expansion matures
  → housing prices AND mortgage rates rise DISPROPORTIONATELY
  → relative housing cost ↑ EVEN AS incomes rise
  → house SALES slow                         (1st: buying turns down)
  → inventory of UNSOLD homes builds         (2nd: the signal)
  → CONSTRUCTION activity turns down         (3rd: real activity follows)
```
⚠️ **Sequencing trap:** buying turns first, construction last, with unsold inventory as the bridge. A stem saying "construction is still strong while unsold inventory is building" describes the *middle* of the turn, not a healthy market.

⚠️ **"Even as household incomes rise"** — affordability can deteriorate in a boom because prices and rates outrun income growth. Rising incomes ≠ improving affordability. (Ratio direction: both numerator and denominator move; the question is which moves faster.)

### Why the links are "far from mechanical" — the late-buyer dynamic 🔴 (the 2008–09 story)
- After rapid past price gains, people **buy for exposure to expected further gains** even when the purchase is otherwise hard to justify → **extends the cycle upward**.
- **"Late buying" invites overbuilding** → large unsold inventory → **downward pressure on prices** → **catches late buyers who stretched their resources** → **more severe correction**.
- This is the curriculum's explanation of the **2008–2009 global financial crisis** housing pattern across many countries.

> **One-liner:** *speculative late buying extends the upswing and deepens the correction, via overbuilding.* Expect the question "a more severe housing correction is *most likely* preceded by: A) rising affordability B) late buying that invites overbuilding C) falling mortgage rates."

> **Two demand sources, apart from affordability (Example 2 Q2 — you got this right):** house buying is driven by **both** the **rate of family formation** (the *actual need* for housing) **and** the **expectation of price increases** (housing's *investment value*). A stem offering one or the other alone is incomplete — the curriculum's answer is "both." Need = demographic trend; investment value = the speculative late-buying dynamic above.

### The role of demographics — the TREND, not the cycle 🟡
- Housing's cyclical behavior occurs **around a long-run growth trend set by demographics** — **family and household formation**.
- Proxy when formation data are missing: **population age structure** — household formation concentrates in the **25- to 40-year-old** group, **adjusted for older people vacating existing homes** → indicator of **underlying, longer-term, secular** housing demand.
- ⚠️ **Scope trap:** demographic measures "have little to do with business cycles." They are a **trend** gauge; what they tell you *about the cycle* is — together with **affordability** — **how quickly the market can correct excess and return to growth**. Trend vs. cycle is the same level-vs-direction discipline as Exhibit 1.

### Impact on the economic cycle — the country contrast 🟡
| Economy type | Demographic driver | Cyclical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Fast-growing developing economies** undergoing **urbanization** (countryside → cities) | Fast growth in housing-unit demand | Housing demand can **quickly reverse cyclical weakness** in the economy |
| **Italy, Japan** | **Net new family formation relatively slight** | Little demographic cushion → housing offers less help in recovery |

## 3. External Trade Sector Behavior (§4.04)
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — the asymmetry "imports follow the DOMESTIC cycle, exports follow FOREIGN cycles" is a reliable question, and the exchange-rate direction calls are your polarity territory**

### Size varies enormously by economy 🟡
| Economy type | Trade share | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Small open economies** — Singapore, Lithuania | **Trade (X + M) can exceed GDP** — almost all inputs imported, bulk of output exported | External conditions — the cycle stage in **key export regions and primary trading partners** — play a crucial role |
| **Large economies** — Brazil, US | Trade is a **much smaller part of GDP** | Domestic cycle dominates |

⚠️ "Trade exceeds GDP" is possible because trade = X + M (gross flows), not net exports. (🔗 M3 §6: only X − M enters GDP.)
- **Since the 1970s** global trade has risen substantially → large economies' business cycles are **more easily transmitted** to other economies.

### The core asymmetry — Exhibit 2 🔴 MEMORIZE
| | Driven by | Behavior through the DOMESTIC cycle |
|---|---|---|
| **Imports** | **DOMESTIC** cycle — rising domestic demand → more purchases from abroad by consumers and businesses | Recovery: **below average, start to increase** → Expansion: **increase** → Slowdown: **peak and start to decline** → Contraction: **decline to below-average** |
| **Exports** | **EXTERNAL** cycles — the rest of the world's demand | **Driven by external demand** — same line across all four domestic phases |

(*Exhibit 2 assumes the exchange rate is unchanged.*)

**Reading the imports row against Exhibit 1:** imports track domestic GDP with a slight lag — they **peak in the slowdown** (when GDP is still above trend), not at the expansion peak. Same lagging shape as employment in the consumer grid. (🔗 M3 §8: M rises with domestic Y — this is that function laid across the phases.)

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED EXAMPLE 3, Q1 HERE
> *"Imports most clearly respond to:"* — you picked **B (domestic industrial policy)**; correct is **C (the domestic GDP growth rate)**.
>
> **Why C:** imports are part of aggregate demand — they reflect **domestic needs for foreign goods**, which **vary with domestic economic growth**. This is the asymmetry rule in its purest form: imports ← domestic cycle.
> **Why B is wrong:** "industrial policy" appears nowhere in the curriculum's model of imports. It *sounds* like a trade-related domestic driver, which is exactly the lure. **Why A is wrong:** exports and imports have *different* drivers (foreign vs. domestic demand) — the whole point of Exhibit 2 is that they don't move together.
>
> **The miss type:** you had the rule ("imports ← domestic cycle") and still picked an option that wasn't in the model. Same failure as Example 1 — a plausible-sounding option beat the stated mechanism. **Fix:** before reading options, write the rule's right-hand side (*"domestic GDP growth"*) and then look for it. If no option matches the rule exactly, pick the closest — never an option the curriculum never mentioned.

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED EXAMPLE 3, Q3 HERE (the portfolio-manager scenario)
> *Three companies: (A) consumer electronics and small vehicles sold locally; (B) equipment sold to domestic companies; (C) household and personal care products sold domestically and abroad. Which fluctuates MOST with the domestic cycle?* — you picked **A**; correct is **B**.
>
> **Classify each company into the cyclicality ranking:**
> | Company | What it really is | Rank |
> |---|---|---|
> | B — equipment sold to domestic companies | **Business investment (I)** | **Most cyclical** |
> | A — electronics, small vehicles | Consumer **durables** | 2nd |
> | C — household & personal care | Consumer **non-durables** (+ partly export-driven, so even less tied to the *domestic* cycle) | Least |
>
> **Why you missed it:** you applied the *consumer-sector* ranking (durables = most cyclical) but truncated it — forgetting the clause from §1 that **"even durables are less cyclical than business investment."** The full ranking has four rungs, and business I sits on top. The curriculum's solution says it flatly: *business investment fluctuates more than consumer spending and exports.*
> **Fix:** when a scenario gives you companies, **translate each into a GDP component first** (C-durable, C-non-durable, I, X) and *then* rank. "Equipment sold to companies" = I, every time. The ranking is `I > durables > services > non-durables`, and I is not optional.

**Consequences 🔴:**
- If foreign cycles are strong, **exports can grow even while the domestic economy's growth declines.**
- To gauge exports, the analyst must assess **the strength of the major trading partners**.
- **Net trade may offset domestic cyclical weakness — and, depending on export importance, could erase it altogether** → the trade balance pattern can be **entirely different from the domestic cycle**.

### The exchange rate — an INDEPENDENT effect 🔴 polarity drill
```
Domestic currency APPRECIATES (gains strength):
   foreign goods cheaper to domestic buyers → IMPORTS ↑ (relative)
   domestic exports pricier abroad           → EXPORTS ↓
   → net exports ↓
Domestic currency DEPRECIATES: opposite → imports ↓, exports ↑, net exports ↑
```
(🔗 M3 §11 Exhibit 14 FX row and §12 — same direction logic; note M3 §12's extra point that a stronger currency also *lowers imported-input costs*, shifting SRAS right. This section only covers the trade-flow side.)

⚠️ **Persistence — the subtle testable point:**
- Currency moves can be **volatile and occasionally extreme**, but they affect trade and the balance of payments **only when they cumulate in a single direction for some time.**
- **Month-to-month or quarter-to-quarter moves, however large, have minimal effect until they persist.**
- **Cumulative moves over years** create trade-flow impacts that **persist even if the currency temporarily reverses.**

> **One-liner:** *size of a currency move matters less than its duration.* Distractor: "a sharp one-quarter depreciation will significantly boost exports" — wrong, it hasn't persisted.

### Overall effect — the two-speed summary 🔴
| Driver | Effect on trade | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| **GDP growth differentials** between countries | **Most immediate and straightforward**: domestic activity ↑/↓ → imports ↑/↓; foreign activity ↑/↓ → exports ↑/↓ | **Fast** |
| **Currency moves** | **More complex** — can reinforce or counteract the cyclical forces | **More gradual** (despite short-term volatility) |

**Analyst workflow:** (1) assess **relative GDP growth rates** at home and abroad → (2) factor in **currency** to see whether it **reinforces or counteracts** the cyclical forces.

## 4. Unemployment (§4.05)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the first calculation of the module (unemployment rate, participation rate), the definitions grid ("who is in the labor force?"), and "unemployment is a LAGGING indicator" are all reliably tested. The discouraged-worker effect on the rate is a guaranteed-style trap.**

### Timing within the cycle 🔴 (lag, both ends)
- **Unemployment is highest somewhat AFTER the recovery has started, and lowest somewhat AFTER the peak.** It lags the cycle at both turning points — consistent with the Exhibit 1 grid (unemployment still above average in recovery, still falling in slowdown).
- Policy backdrop: governments and central banks target **limiting unemployment** and **containing inflation** (preserving purchasing power).

### The price–wage spiral 🟡 (links to inflation section ahead)
```
Very low unemployment (overheated economy)
  → workers expect prices to keep rising AND have market power (few spare workers)
  → wage demands ↑  +  wage ESCALATOR clauses (automatic rises indexed to CPI)
  → employers raise prices IN ADVANCE to protect margins
  → price–wage inflationary spiral, growing with time ("avalanche")
```
- **Inflation expectations are the key mechanism** — high expectations → stronger wage demands → pre-emptive price rises.
- Problem of the **1960s–70s** industrialized countries; still relevant.
- **Central bank response:** slow the economy, sometimes drastically, to **reset inflation expectations at a low level** (if everyone expects low inflation, the spiral stops) — this **may trigger a deep recession**.
- 🔴 **Analyst takeaway:** signs of a price–wage spiral → consider **both** high inflation **and sharp monetary tightening**. (🔗 M3 §13 inflationary gap: SRAS shifts left as input prices get bid up; 🔗 M3 §14: rates ↑ → cut long-maturity bonds.) Labor-market measures therefore help assess **risk of cyclical downturn**.

### The definitions grid 🔴 — "who counts where?"
| Term | Definition | In labor force? | Counted as unemployed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Employed** | Has a job (excludes the **informal sector** — street vendors, illegal workers) | Yes | No |
| **Unemployed** | **Actively seeking** work, currently without a job | Yes | **Yes** |
| → Long-term unemployed | Out of work a long time (**> 3–4 months** in many countries) but **still looking** | Yes | Yes |
| → Frictionally unemployed | Searching for a better match, or **between jobs** (voluntarily left; new entrants / re-entrants not yet placed) — **short-term and transitory** | Yes | Yes |
| **Underemployed** | Has a job but is **qualified for a significantly higher-paying one**, or **part-time but wants full-time** (the lawyer in the bookstore) | Yes | **NO — she has a job** |
| **Discouraged worker** | Has **stopped looking** (gave up, e.g., weak economy) | **NO** — statistically outside, like children and retirees | **NO** |
| **Voluntarily unemployed** | Voluntarily outside the labor force — refuses an available vacancy below their wage threshold; early retirees | **NO** | No |
| Retirees, children, stay-at-home parents, full-time students | Neither employed nor seeking | No | No |

⚠️ **Hidden unemployment** = discouraged workers + the underemployed. Neither appears in the official unemployment rate — one is outside the labor force, the other is counted as *employed*.
⚠️ **Underemployment is hard to classify:** the lawyer may simply *prefer* the bookstore — which is why the statistic is criticized but not adjusted.

### The two ratios 🔴 CALCULATION
```
Unemployment rate  =  Unemployed / Labor force
Participation rate =  Labor force / Working-age population     (working age ≈ 16–64)
                      (also called the "activity ratio")
```
⚠️ **Denominator trap:** the unemployment rate is a share of the **labor force**, NOT of the population. The participation rate is a share of the **working-age population**, NOT the total population.

**Worked example (verified) — the discouraged-worker effect:**
```
Working-age population 100m | Labor force 65m | Employed 60m | Unemployed 5m
  UR = 5 / 65  = 7.69%        PR = 65 / 100 = 65.0%

Recession deepens: 1m unemployed give up searching (become discouraged)
  Labor force 64m | Unemployed 4m
  UR = 4 / 64  = 6.25%  ↓      PR = 64 / 100 = 64.0%  ↓
  → the unemployment rate FELL while the economy got WORSE

Recovery: 2m discouraged workers resume searching but aren't hired yet
  Labor force 66m | Unemployed 6m
  UR = 6 / 66  = 9.09%  ↑      PR = 66 / 100 = 66.0%  ↑
  → the unemployment rate ROSE while the economy got BETTER
```
> 🔴 **The rule:** read the **participation rate together with the unemployment rate**. UR ↓ with PR ↓ = discouraged workers leaving, not improvement. UR ↑ with PR ↑ = re-entrants returning, i.e., improvement. *(Polarity item: the rate's direction alone can point the wrong way — you need the second ratio to sign it.)*

### Measurement differences across countries 🟡
- **US:** monthly **household survey** by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (how many have jobs; how many of working age are seeking).
- Others: **claims for unemployment assistance**; or defining the labor force as **everyone of working age** regardless of willingness to work.
- → International comparisons are problematic; **ILO statistics** use consistent methodologies.
- ⚠️ The working-age-population approach **avoids the discouraged-worker bias** (the denominator doesn't shrink with conditions) but adds its own — it counts people with **severe disabilities who could never work** as unemployed.

### Why unemployment is a LAGGING indicator — two reasons 🔴
| Reason | Mechanism | Effect on the rate |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Backward-looking labor force** | The labor force **expands and contracts with the economy**: discouraged workers leave in hard times (rate looks better than reality) and return when the market improves — but seldom find work at once (rate looks worse than reality) | Can make the rate **rise even as recovery gains momentum** |
| **2. Business reluctance to lay off / rehire** | Firms retain good workers (and labor contracts make layoffs expensive) → unemployment **rises more slowly** into recession. In recovery, firms **first fully use the workers they kept** before hiring → **declines lag** too | Lag at both turning points |

### Payroll, hours, temps, and productivity — the EARLY signals 🔴
**Payroll employment:** counting payroll size **sidesteps the discouraged-worker ebb and flow**. Bias: **hard to count small businesses**, which may drive employment growth. Still: payrolls shrink = clear trouble; payrolls rise = clear recovery.

**The sequencing — same mechanism as the Exhibit 1 labor row, now as indicators:**
```
DOWNTURN:  productivity ↓ (output falls, payroll kept)
           → hours cut, especially OVERTIME
           → part-time & TEMPORARY staff cut           ← strong weakness signal
           → full-time layoffs                          ← LAST
UPSWING:   productivity ↑ (underemployed workers reused)
           → overtime & hours ↑
           → TEMPORARY staffing ↑                       ← recovery signal, "long before"
           → full-time rehiring                         ← LAST
```
- Firms **don't want to make mistakes with full-time staff** in either direction → hours and temps are the adjustable margins.
- Overtime cuts alone may be month-to-month noise; **confirmed by temp cutbacks + other independent indicators** = strong signal.

**Productivity = output / hours worked.** Because firms keep workers as output falls, measured productivity **drops first** — potentially **even before hours change**, and certainly **before any change in full-time payrolls**. Symmetrically, it **rises first** in recovery as underemployed workers are put back to use.

> **Leading-to-lagging order for labor indicators:** productivity → hours/overtime → temporary staff → full-time payrolls → **unemployment rate (lagging)**.

**Phase signatures from the labor data (Example 4 Q3 — you got this right):**
| Labor-market reading | Phase it signals |
|---|---|
| UR high **and rising**, hours ↓, productivity ↓ | **Deepening recession** — layoffs underway but smaller than the output drop (turnover is costly) → productivity falls |
| UR still high, but **overtime ↑, productivity ↑** | **End of recession** — firms hesitant to hire, so they work retained staff harder first |
| UR low, hours high | **Peak** |

⚠️ The discriminator between "deepening" and "ending" is **hours and productivity**, not the unemployment rate — the rate is lagging in both cases. And at the peak, with the market tight, the people still unemployed are mostly **frictional** (between jobs, new entrants) — Example 4 Q1.

⚠️ **Scope trap — structural productivity:** productivity gains from **technology breakthroughs or better training** affect **potential GDP** (🔗 M3 §12 LRAS) and can **slow employment trends** (less staff needed per unit of output) — but these **unfold over decades** and "mean little to cyclical considerations" (which play out over years). Few statistical indicators exist for them; analysts rely on anecdote and longitudinal studies. Trend vs. cycle, again.

## 5. Inflation (§4.06)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the module's biggest section. Guaranteed items: the price-index calculation (Laspeyres), the three biases (all upward), deflation vs. disinflation, headline vs. core, cost-push vs. demand-pull, and the TIPS breakeven calculation. Plus a polarity minefield: velocity, NAIRU, and the money-growth signals.**

### Definitions and timing 🔴
- **Inflation** = a **sustained** rise in the overall (aggregate) price level. **Inflation rate** = the **% change in a price index** — the *speed* of price-level movement.
- **Pro-cyclical with a lag of a year or more** — rises and falls *with* the cycle, late. (Compare: unemployment is counter-cyclical and lagging; both lag.)
- Why investors care: (1) infers the state of the economy; (2) an **unexpected change may trigger a monetary-policy change** → large, immediate asset-price impact; (3) in developing countries very high inflation → social unrest / political shifts = **political risk**.
- **Monetary policy** = determines interest rates and the quantities of money and loans. Central banks watch domestic inflation closely.

| Combination | Diagnosis | Policy response |
|---|---|---|
| High inflation + **fast growth + low unemployment** | **Overheating** | Policy moves to cool it down |
| High inflation + **high unemployment + slowdown** | **Stagflation** (stagnation + inflation) | **Left to correct itself** — no short-term policy deemed effective (🔗 M3 §13 Case 4) |

### Deflation, hyperinflation, disinflation 🔴 definition trap
| Term | Definition | Price level | Inflation rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Deflation** | Sustained **decrease** in aggregate price level | **Falling** | **< 0%** |
| **Hyperinflation** | Extremely fast increase (e.g., **500–1,000% per year**) | Exploding | Extremely high |
| **Disinflation** | A **decline in the inflation rate** (e.g., 15–20% → 5–6%) | **Still RISING**, just slower | **Still positive** |

⚠️ **Disinflation ≠ deflation.** After disinflation the price level *keeps rising*. The distractor: "disinflation means prices are falling" — wrong. (Level vs. rate-of-change — same discipline as the phase grid.) ✓ Example 5 Q1.

⚠️ **Which can be identified from the inflation rate ALONE?** Deflation (rate < 0) and hyperinflation (rate extreme) — yes. **Stagflation — NO**: it requires high inflation **together with high unemployment / slow growth**, a second variable. ✓ Example 5 Q3.

⚠️ **One good's price ≠ the inflation rate.** A rise in the price of chicken does not raise inflation by itself — modern baskets hold many goods, so a single product rarely moves the overall level. (Example 5 Q1, distractor C.)

**Why deflation is dangerous (the mechanism, testable):**
```
Deflation → value of money ↑ → debt contracts are FIXED nominal amounts
  → borrowers' liabilities RISE in real terms
  → company revenues fall as prices fall (in recession)
  → cash-short firms cut spending, investment, workforce SHARPLY
  → less spending + high unemployment → contraction deepens
```
- Hence the **~2% preferred inflation rate** consensus for developed economies — a buffer from deflation.
- Episodes: US Great Depression; briefly after the 2008–09 crisis; **Japan since the late 1990s** (several episodes).

**Hyperinflation — causes and cure:**
- Cause: **large-scale government spending not backed by tax revenue, accommodated by the monetary authority printing money**; also supply shortages from war, regime transition, or prolonged political instability.
- Dynamic: people rush to convert cash into real goods → money changes hands at **extremely high frequency** (velocity ↑) → more cash chasing limited goods → price rises accelerate.
- 🔴 **Cure:** the basic cause is **too much money in circulation** → **regaining control of the money supply** is the key to ending it.

### Constructing a price index — Exhibit 3 🔴 CALCULATION (verified)
A price index = average prices of a **basket**, weighted by each good's relative importance.

| | Jan 2019 (base) | | Feb 2019 | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Qty | Price | Qty | Price |
| Rice | 50 kg | ¥3/kg | 70 kg | ¥4/kg |
| Gasoline | 70 L | ¥4.4/L | 60 L | ¥4.5/L |

```
Base basket at base prices:  (50 × 3) + (70 × 4.4) = ¥458
SAME basket at Feb prices:   (50 × 4) + (70 × 4.5) = ¥515     ← hold Jan QUANTITIES fixed

Price index (base = 100):  515 / 458 × 100 = 112.45
Inflation rate:            112.45 / 100 − 1 = 12.45%
```
⚠️ **The quantity trap:** the index uses **base-period quantities** with **current prices**. Using Feb quantities (70 kg, 60 L) in both periods gives a different number (≈116.0) — that's a Paasche index, not what the curriculum computes. Read which quantities are held fixed. (🔗 M3 §5: the GDP deflator is the reverse — *current* quantities at base prices. Don't cross the two.)

**Laspeyres index** = basket composition **held constant** at base period. Most indexes worldwide are Laspeyres because basket survey data arrive **with a lag**; many countries update the basket **every five years**.

### The three biases of a fixed basket 🔴 — ALL UPWARD
| Bias | Mechanism | Direction | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Substitution bias** | Price of a good rises → people switch to cheaper substitutes; fixed basket ignores this | **Upward** | **Chained price index** formula (only "somewhat" resolves it) |
| **Quality bias** | Same product improves over time (cars: safer, more reliable) while its price rises; unadjusted, the price rise overstates inflation | **Upward** | **Hedonic pricing** (adjust for quality) |
| **New product bias** | New products aren't in the fixed basket | **Upward** | Introduce new products into the basket over time |

> **One-liner:** *a fixed-basket (Laspeyres) index OVERSTATES inflation for three reasons — substitution, quality, new products.* Memory hook: **S-Q-N, all up.** Quality and new-product biases are "relatively easy" to fix; substitution is harder.

### Cost-push: the labor market and NAIRU 🔴
- Commodities are an input, but **wages are the single biggest cost** → focus on **wage-push** inflation.

**NAIRU / NARU 🔴 definition:**
- **Not all labor is alike.** Structural factors — **training deficiencies, cultural patterns, labor-market inefficiencies** — mean shortages emerge **long before the unemployment rate reaches very low figures**.
- The **effective unemployment rate below which labor-market pressure emerges** = **NAIRU** (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment) = **NARU** (natural rate of unemployment, after **Milton Friedman**). Same concept, two names.
- **Varies across economies and over time.** ⚠️ **It is NAIRU — not "full employment" — that determines when bottlenecks and wage-push pressure arrive.** (🔗 M3 §10: the natural rate behind LRAS.)
- **What NAIRU depends on (Example 8 Q7 ✓):** the **skill set of the labor force relative to the economy's industrial mix** — if a large part of the workforce can't meet employers' needs, NAIRU is **high**. Not past cyclical swings, not union bargaining power.

### Unit labor cost 🔴 FORMULA
```
ULC = W / O
  W = total labor compensation per hour per worker
  O = output per hour per worker (productivity)
```
- Higher productivity → compensation is spread over more units → **lower price needed per unit**. Faster productivity growth → compensation can rise faster **without** cost pressure.
- In growth rates: **%ΔULC ≈ %ΔW − %ΔO** (🔗 M3 §12 — same formula; if wages +5% and productivity +5%, no pressure).
- **The inflationary case:** compensation outruns productivity → ULC ↑ → firms protect margins → **prices under upward pressure**. Usually because heavy labor demand relative to available labor pushed compensation faster than productivity.
- Productivity drivers: cyclical swings (§4), technology, training, and **the pace of development** (better equipment and systems → higher output per hour).

### Demand-pull: capacity, commodities, and the Monetarist view 🔴
- Indicators: **actual vs. potential GDP** and **capacity utilization**. Closer to potential / higher utilization → shortages, bottlenecks → **price increases**. Further below potential → **slowdown in inflation or deflation**. (🔗 M3 §11: 82–85% utilization triggers; §13 inflationary gap.)
- **Commodity prices** — partly a cost, but mainly a **sign of excess demand**. ⚠️ Can **mislead** for a single economy: commodities trade **globally**, so they reflect global conditions more than domestic ones.

**Monetarist argument:** inflation is **fundamentally a monetary phenomenon** — "**too much money chasing too few goods.**" A surplus of money lowers its value (like any surplus) → expressed as higher prices. ⚠️ **Not a separate theory in practice:** excess money → more liquidity → rapid rise in demand → the Monetarist view is a **special case of demand-pull**; the distinction is only in **identifying the initial cause** of the demand excess.

### Inflation expectations 🔴
- Once inflation is **embedded**, every actor builds it into their actions → **self-sustaining momentum** → inflation **persists even after its initial cause has disappeared**. (This is the §4 price–wage spiral's engine.)
- History: 1970s–early 1980s Europe and US — high inflation persisted on expectations **even after recession** → slow/negative growth + high unemployment + rising inflation = **stagflation**.

**Measuring expectations (hard):**
1. **Past inflation trends** — assume participants extrapolate
2. **Surveys** — often biased by how questions are asked
3. **Inflation-linked bonds (TIPS)** — 🔴 the calculation

```
Breakeven (market-implied) inflation ≈ Nominal bond yield − Inflation-protected bond yield
e.g., 10-yr nominal 3.5%  −  10-yr TIPS 1.5%  =  2.0% average annual inflation expected over 10 years
```

Flow = now-negative; stock = later-positive

── HOUSING (§4.03) ──
Small share of GDP, DRAMATIC swings → outsized cyclical impact
Property values ↔ consumer wealth & confidence (US, UK)
Stats: unsold-home inventory | avg/median price (per unit or per m²)
Rates ↓ → buying ↑ → construction ↑  (mortgage-financed → rate-sensitive)
Affordability = household income vs (house price + typical mortgage cost)
CREDIT-CYCLE SEQUENCE as expansion matures:
  prices & mortgage rates rise disproportionately → relative cost ↑
  EVEN AS incomes rise → SALES slow → UNSOLD INVENTORY builds
  → CONSTRUCTION turns down   (buying first, construction last)
Late buying (for expected gains) → extends upswing → OVERBUILDING
  → inventory → price pressure → catches stretched late buyers
  → MORE SEVERE correction  (2008–09 pattern)
Demographics = TREND (household formation, age 25–40 adjusted for
  older vacating) — "little to do with business cycles"; with
  affordability, gauges how fast the market corrects excess
Urbanizing developing economies: housing demand can reverse weakness
  quickly; Italy/Japan: slight family formation → little cushion

── EXTERNAL TRADE (§4.04) ──
Small open economies (Singapore, Lithuania): X + M can EXCEED GDP
  → trading partners' cycle stage is crucial
Large economies (Brazil, US): trade a small share of GDP
Since 1970s: more trade → cycles transmit more easily across borders
THE ASYMMETRY: ⭐ (missed the imports side)
  IMPORTS ← DOMESTIC GDP GROWTH (below avg → rise → PEAK in slowdown
            → decline to below avg)       [FX assumed unchanged]
  EXPORTS ← EXTERNAL demand (foreign cycles), all four phases
Exports can grow while domestic growth falls; net trade can offset
  or even ERASE domestic weakness → trade pattern ≠ domestic cycle
FX (independent effect):
  APPRECIATION → imports ↑, exports ↓, net exports ↓
  DEPRECIATION → imports ↓, exports ↑, net exports ↑
  Matters only when CUMULATIVE in one direction over time;
  single-month/quarter moves (however big) ≈ minimal effect;
  multi-year moves persist even through temporary reversals
Two speeds: GDP growth differentials = immediate & straightforward;
  currency = more complex, more GRADUAL
Workflow: relative GDP growth first → then FX (reinforce/counteract?)

── UNEMPLOYMENT (§4.05) ──
Timing: unemployment HIGHEST somewhat AFTER recovery starts,
        LOWEST somewhat AFTER the peak → LAGGING indicator
Price–wage spiral: low unemployment → wage demands ↑ (expectations +
  market power) + escalator clauses → pre-emptive price rises → spiral;
  CB resets expectations by slowing economy → may cause deep recession
  → analyst: expect high inflation AND sharp tightening
WHO COUNTS WHERE:
  Employed (excl. informal sector) | Unemployed = actively seeking, no job
  Long-term (>3–4 months, still looking) | Frictional (between jobs,
    new/re-entrants; short-term, transitory)
  UNDEREMPLOYED = has job (overqualified / part-time wanting full-time)
    → counted EMPLOYED, not unemployed
  DISCOURAGED = stopped looking → OUTSIDE labor force, not counted
  Voluntarily unemployed = outside LF (refuses low wage; early retiree)
  Hidden unemployment = discouraged + underemployed
RATIOS:
  Unemployment rate  = Unemployed / LABOR FORCE
  Participation rate = Labor force / WORKING-AGE population (16–64)
  e.g., LF 65, U 5, WA 100 → UR 7.69%, PR 65%
  1m discouraged leave → U 4/LF 64 → UR 6.25% ↓, PR 64% ↓  (worse, not better)
  2m re-enter unhired → U 6/LF 66 → UR 9.09% ↑, PR 66% ↑  (better, not worse)
  → ALWAYS read UR together with PR
Measurement: US = BLS household survey; others = benefit claims or
  working-age pop as LF (avoids discouraged bias, but counts the
  permanently disabled); ILO for consistent comparisons
WHY LAGGING (two reasons): (1) labor force expands/contracts with
  the economy (discouraged flow); (2) firms reluctant to lay off,
  then use retained workers fully before rehiring
EARLY SIGNALS (leading → lagging):
  productivity (output/hours) → hours & OVERTIME → TEMPORARY staff
  → full-time payrolls → unemployment rate
  Payrolls sidestep discouraged-worker flow; bias = small firms hard to count
  Structural productivity (tech, training) = potential GDP, decades,
  "means little to cyclical considerations"

── INFLATION (§4.06) ──
Inflation = SUSTAINED rise in price level; rate = %Δ price index
PRO-cyclical, lag of a year or more
Overheating = high infl + fast growth + low UR → policy cools it
Stagflation = high infl + high UR + slowdown → left to self-correct
DEFLATION: rate < 0, price level FALLING; debt fixed nominal → real
  liabilities ↑ → firms slash spending → contraction deepens;
  ~2% target as buffer; US 1930s, 2008–09; Japan since late 1990s
HYPERINFLATION: 500–1,000%/yr; govt spending unbacked by tax +
  money printing; cure = regain control of MONEY SUPPLY
DISINFLATION: rate FALLS but stays POSITIVE — prices still RISING
PRICE INDEX (Laspeyres = base quantities held fixed):
  idx_t = Σ(q_base × p_t) / Σ(q_base × p_base) × 100
  Exhibit 3: 458 → 515 → idx 112.45 → inflation 12.45%
  (⚠ base QUANTITIES, current PRICES — opposite of GDP deflator)
  Basket updated ~every 5 years (survey lag)
THREE BIASES — ALL UPWARD (S-Q-N): substitution (fix: chained index),
  quality (fix: hedonic pricing), new product (fix: add to basket)
  ⭐ trigger word "FIXED BASKET" → OVERSTATES cost of living, always
  ⭐ bias = CONSTRUCTION methodology; NOT about which goods included
Stagflation needs TWO variables (inflation + unemployment) — not
  identifiable from the inflation rate alone; deflation/hyper are
INDEXES: CPI (food weight higher in developing economies);
  US CPI-U = urban only, household survey, BLS/Labor;
  US PCE = all consumption, business surveys, BEA/Commerce,
    Fed switched to it in 2000 (CPI-U biases + fuller coverage);
  HICP = ECB, Eurostat aggregates; WPI = RBI (food 27% vs 70%
    rural CPI; no retail markups → understates);
  PPI = producer prices, crude→intermediate→finished, feeds FUTURE
    CPI, cross-country weights differ a lot; aka WPI
  TIPS principal ← CPI-U; contracts/leases ← CPI; business ← PPI
HEADLINE = all items | CORE = ex food & energy (policy makers'
  trend read, avoid overreacting to short-term swings)
  ⭐ headline vs core = more NOISE / short-term conditions — NOT "bias"
INDUSTRY ANALYSIS ⭐: sales/output prices → CPI sub-index;
  production costs/inputs → WPI/PPI sub-index; need BOTH for both
Deflating nominal GDP → GDP DEFLATOR (domestic OUTPUT prices),
  not CPI/PCE (consumption baskets ≠ output mix)
TWO TYPES: cost-push (costs→prices, usually wages) | demand-pull
  (demand→prices→wages). Same root: excess demand > capacity
INDICATOR MAP ⭐ (label the type FIRST, then pull the data):
  COST-PUSH   → UR vs NAIRU; wage growth vs productivity (ULC)
  DEMAND-PULL → money growth vs nominal GDP; actual vs potential; capacity
  EXPECTATIONS→ past trends; surveys; TIPS breakeven
  Commodities → either, limited info (global)
Cost-push occurs when unemployment is LOW (below NAIRU) — one
  direction, never "either" ⭐ hedge options are distractors
Monetarist = MONEY GROWTH ("always and everywhere monetary") —
  NOT interest rates ⭐
NAIRU = NARU (Friedman) = effective UR below which wage pressure
  emerges; structural (training, culture, inefficiency); varies by
  economy & time; THIS, not full employment, sets the bottleneck
  Depends on: SKILL SET of labor force vs industrial mix
  ⭐ Limitations (TRUE): not directly observable | varies over time
     | misses segment bottlenecks
     NOT a limitation: "only works in monetarist models" — no school
  ⭐ NOT/EXCEPT/least-likely stems: mark each option T/F, pick the F
ULC = W / O   (compensation per hour ÷ output per hour)
  compensation > productivity → ULC ↑ → price pressure
DEMAND-PULL indicators: actual vs potential GDP, capacity utilization;
  commodities = excess-demand sign but GLOBAL → can mislead
MONETARIST: "too much money chasing too few goods" — a SPECIAL CASE
  of demand-pull (differs only in initial cause)
  Money growth > nominal GDP growth (esp. accelerating) → inflationary
  Money growth < economy (esp. decelerating) → disinflation/deflation
VELOCITY = nominal GDP / money supply
  stable → price stability
  falls via GDP ↓ (cyclical) → upswing likely, NOT inflation
  falls via money ↑ → INFLATIONARY
  rises → money shortage → disinflation/deflation
  ⚠ ask WHICH side moved
EXPECTATIONS: embedded → self-sustaining → persists after cause gone
  (1970s–80s stagflation). Measures: past trends | surveys (biased)
  | TIPS breakeven = nominal yield − TIPS yield (3.5 − 1.5 = 2.0%)
    caveat: small market, pension-fund demand distorts
