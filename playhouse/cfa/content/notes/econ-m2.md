## 1. The Four Market Structures — the Master Framework
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the backbone of the entire reading**

```
Perfect competition → Monopolistic competition → Oligopoly → Monopoly
   (most competitive)                                (least competitive)
```

**Memorize this ordering.** Almost every question in this reading is: *"Here is a described market — which structure is it, and what follows?"*

### Definitions worth one line each 🟢
- **Market** = buyers and sellers **aware of each other** who can **agree on a price**.
- **Geographic scope:** if a service can be **digitized**, its market is **worldwide** (internet search, radiology reads); physical constraints keep markets **local** (premixed cement).

### Concentrated vs. fragmented markets 🟡
- **Concentrated:** majority of sales from a **few firms** (internet search: 3 firms ≈ 98.9% of US market, 2018; iPod >70% world MP3 share, 2009).
- **Fragmented:** many small independents (automobile repairs).
- This intuition feeds the concentration measures (CR, HHI) later in the reading.

## 2. The Four Structures Defined
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — identify each from a one-line description**

### 2.1 Perfect Competition
- Profits driven down to the **normal profit** — the required return / rental cost of capital.
- ⚠️ **Zero economic profit ≠ zero accounting profit.** Perfectly competitive firms are NOT doomed to extinction — they earn their required return. Favorite distractor.
- Found in **commodities markets**: strictly **homogeneous product**, no producer large enough to influence price.
- 🔗 M1 §10.2: this firm faces a **horizontal (perfectly elastic)** demand curve, so **MR = P**.

### 2.2 Monopolistic Competition
"Competition with a twist of monopoly":
- **Many firms** (the competition part) + **product differentiation** (the monopoly part) → **some pricing power** from convincing consumers of unique characteristics.
- **Memory hook:** many firms, differentiated products, a little pricing power (restaurants, clothing brands).

### 2.3 Oligopoly
- **Few firms** supplying the market.
- 🔴 **Defining feature — interdependence:** each firm must anticipate **rivals' retaliatory strategies** when changing price or output.
- **Memory hook:** the only structure where firms play **chess against each other**. Question stem mentions rivals' *reactions* → oligopoly.

### 2.4 Monopoly
- **Single seller**, **no close substitute**, considerable pricing power — *if unconstrained*.
- ⚠️ **Regulation caveat (heavily testable):** most real monopolies are **regulated**; the regulator sets prices to allow a **normal return** on investment.

## 3. The Five Factors That Determine Market Structure 🔴

1. **Number and relative size** of firms
2. Degree of **product differentiation**
3. Seller's **power over pricing**
4. Strength of **barriers to entry and exit**
5. Degree of **non-price competition**

**Memory hook: N-D-P-B-N** — *Number, Differentiation, Pricing power, Barriers, Non-price.*

## 4. Exhibit 1 — The Characteristics Table 🔴 MEMORIZE COLD
**The most efficient set of exam points in the reading.**

| Market Structure | Sellers | Differentiation | Barriers | Pricing Power | Non-price Competition |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Perfect competition** | Many | Homogeneous | **Very Low** | **None** | **None** |
| **Monopolistic competition** | Many | **Differentiated** | Low | Some | Advertising **and** Differentiation |
| **Oligopoly** | **Few** | Homogeneous | **High** | Some/Considerable | Advertising and Differentiation |
| **Monopoly** | **One** | **Unique** | **Very High** | Considerable | Advertising only |

**Row logic beats 24 cells:** moving down — sellers ↓, barriers ↑, pricing power ↑, all monotonic **except two trap columns**:
- ⚠️ **Differentiation:** perfect competition AND **oligopoly are both homogeneous**; only monopolistic competition is "differentiated." Classic trick pairs them.
- ⚠️ **Non-price competition:** perfect competition has **none** (why advertise a commodity?); monopoly has **advertising only** (nothing to differentiate against); the middle two use **both**.

**One-line identifiers:**
- Many + no pricing power + homogeneous → **perfect competition**
- Many + differentiated + some power → **monopolistic competition**
- Few + watching rivals + high barriers → **oligopoly**
- One + unique + no substitutes → **monopoly**

## 6. The Innovation Trade-off 🟡
- Perfect competition delivers the **largest quantity at the lowest price** — but other structures may **fund more innovation**.
- **Clinical trials** would bankrupt perfectly competitive firms but are affordable under **oligopoly**.
- 🔴 **Exam-ready conclusion:** *consumers can benefit from less-than-perfectly-competitive markets.* An answer choice claiming perfect competition is unambiguously best for consumers is wrong for exactly this reason.

## 8. Perfect Competition — The Five Conditions 🔴
1. **Large number of potential buyers AND sellers** (note: *potential* — threat of entry counts)
2. **Virtually identical** products
3. **Few / easily surmountable barriers** to entry and exit
4. **No market-pricing power**
5. **Non-price competition absent**

= the perfect-competition **row of Exhibit 1** read left to right. One memorization, two question types.

### Benchmark role 🟡
**Few real markets qualify.** Its value is as the **reference outcome** other structures are compared against.

### Canonical example — corn for animal feed 🔴
| Condition | How corn satisfies it |
|---|---|
| Identical products | Farmer Brown's bushel = Farmer Lopez's bushel |
| Cheap information | Active **futures and spot markets** |
| No non-price competition | **No way to differentiate**, e.g., by advertising |
| Low barriers | Capital intensive, **but** with abundant land/water, barriers are **relatively low** |

⚠️ **"Capital intensive" alone ≠ high barriers** — a subtle distractor.

## 9b. NEW Elasticity Material (the Market-Structures layer)
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — everything below is new; the basics are M1 §4–6.**

> 🔗 M1 covers: income elasticity, cross-price elasticity, signs, movement vs. shift, elasticity ↔ TR table. Only the additions appear here.

### ⚠️ Sign convention alert
This reading treats **εp as a positive magnitude** (elastic means εp > 1). Module 1 kept the negative sign and compared |Ed| to 1. Same concept, different notation — **check which convention the question uses.** *This is exactly your known sign-handling error pattern — slow down here.*

### Cross-price elasticity as a TEST OF COMPETITION 🔴 NEW
| Substitutes are... | Pricing power |
|---|---|
| **More numerous and closer** | **Lower** |
| **Fewer** | **Greater** |

**Memory hook:** many close substitutes = customers can escape = no pricing power. This is the quantitative version of Exhibit 1's pricing-power column.

### TR is maximized where MR = 0 🔴 NEW — reproduce the logic
1. While **MR > 0**, each extra unit adds to TR → TR rising
2. When **MR < 0**, TR declines — the %price drop now exceeds the %quantity gain
3. Therefore **TR peaks exactly where MR = 0**

🔗 M1: MR = 0 at the **midpoint of a linear demand curve** = the **unit-elastic** point = revenue-maximizing quantity. Three facts, one location.

### 🔴 NEW FORMULA — MR and price elasticity
```
MR = P × [ 1 − (1/εp) ]
```
MR = price, discounted by a factor that depends on elasticity:

| Elasticity | 1/εp | MR | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| εp → ∞ (perfect competition) | → 0 | **MR = P** | No price cut needed to sell more |
| εp > 1 (elastic) | < 1 | **MR > 0** | Selling more adds revenue |
| εp = 1 (unitary) | = 1 | **MR = 0** | TR at maximum |
| εp < 1 (inelastic) | > 1 | **MR < 0** | Selling more destroys revenue |

**Worked example:** P = €10, εp = 2 → MR = 10 × [1 − ½] = **€5**. Same P, εp = 1.25 → MR = 10 × [1 − 0.8] = **€2**. Less elastic → lower MR.

> ⚠️ **Tested constantly:** a profit-maximizing firm **never knowingly operates on the inelastic portion** of its demand curve (MR < 0 there: cutting output raises revenue AND cuts costs). Corollary: a **monopolist always prices in the elastic region**.

**Strategic takeaway 🟡:** elasticity predicts a price change's effect on TR *in advance* — inelastic region: raise price → TR ↑; elastic region: raise price → TR ↓.

## 9c. Consumer Surplus: Value minus Expenditure
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — concept ("what does the demand curve measure?") + calculation (triangle area). Also the tool for later welfare/deadweight-loss analysis.**

### Definition
```
Consumer surplus = Total value to buyer − Total expenditure
```
The gap between the **maximum you'd have paid** and what you **actually paid** — the "bargain."

### The key reinterpretation 🔴
**The demand curve IS a marginal value curve** — it shows the highest price consumers would pay **for each additional unit** (willingness to pay = value measured in goods sacrificed; value per unit falls as consumption rises).

> **One-liner:** *the demand curve is consumers' willingness to pay for each additional unit.*

### Geometry (Exhibit 3) — buyer purchases Q₁ at P₁
| Area | What it is | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Total value** | Sum of marginal values of all units | **Entire area under demand curve** to Q₁ |
| **Total expenditure** | What's paid | **Rectangle** P₁ × Q₁ |
| **Consumer surplus** | Value − expenditure | **Triangle** below demand curve, **above the price line** |

**Why the triangle exists:** the Q₁-th unit's marginal value = P₁ exactly (zero surplus on the last unit); every earlier unit was worth **more** than P₁ but cost only P₁.

### The calculation (linear demand) ⚠️ exam version
```
Consumer surplus = ½ × Q₁ × (Pmax − P₁)

Pmax = the PRICE (vertical) intercept of the demand curve
```

**Worked example (M1's inverse demand P = 8.92 − 0.156Q):** at P₁ = €1.48, Q₁ = 47.54:
```
CS = ½ × 47.54 × (8.92 − 1.48) = ½ × 47.54 × 7.44 ≈ €176.8
```

⚠️ **Common slips:** (1) using the **quantity** intercept for the height, (2) forgetting the **½**, (3) computing **total value** when asked for **surplus** or vice versa — read whether the stem says *value*, *expenditure*, or *surplus*.

## 9d. Supply, Costs, and Short-Run Profit in Perfect Competition
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — three guaranteed items: economic vs. accounting profit, the equilibrium calc, P = AR = MR.**

### Firm supply → market supply 🟡
- Individual firm supply curves slope **up** (higher price → more supplied, but at rising cost: less productive land, more irrigation/fertilizer).
- 🔴 **Aggregation rule: market supply = SUM of individual firms' supply curves** (same horizontal-summing as market demand, 🔗 M1 §6b).
- *Exhibit 4:* at P = 5, Brown 7 + Lopez 5 + others = industry 35.

### Economic vs. accounting profit 🔴 GUARANTEED
| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Economic costs** | ALL remuneration needed to **keep a resource in its current employment** |
| **Opportunity cost** | The resource's **next best opportunity** (🔗 M1 §9.1 seeded this) |
| **Economic profit** | TR − TC, where TC includes the **full normal return on ALL resources** |
| **Accounting profit** | **Explicit payments** only (workers, vendors, lenders) + **historic-cost depreciation** |

**Two opportunity-cost examples (distractor material):**
1. **Farmer's own labor:** could earn USD 50,000 elsewhere → that's a cost even though no check is written.
2. **Farmer's own land/capital:** could be leased or sold and reinvested → forgone return is a cost.

⚠️ Expect: *Economic profit = Accounting profit − unrecorded opportunity costs.* And: **zero economic profit still means the owner earns a normal return on everything.**

### The equilibrium calculation 🔴 under one minute
```
Supply:  Qs = 10 + 5P    inverted:  P = −2 + 0.2Q
Demand (inverse):  P = 25 − 0.5Q

25 − 0.5Q = −2 + 0.2Q     [equate the P expressions]
27 = 0.7Q*  →  Q* = 38.57
P* = 25 − 0.5(38.57) = 5.71
```
⚠️ **Method note:** both functions in **inverse (P = ...) form → equate P expressions**. M1 §3's version (Qd = 50 − 2P) is in **direct form → equate Q expressions**. Check which form the question gives you.

### The punchline: market price BECOMES the firm's demand curve 🔴
- The equilibrium price (5.71) is a **horizontal line** = each firm's demand function.
- ⚠️ **Definitional test:** *if any one firm could change the equilibrium market price, the market is NOT perfectly competitive.* The cleanest one-liner the exam can quote.

### Revenue schedules of the price taker 🔴
```
P = AR = MR        (one horizontal line, triple duty)
```
- **AR = the firm's demand function** (true in *every* structure).
- TR = P × Q = the rectangle under the flat AR line.

### Cost side — corn specifics only
🔗 M1 §9.4/§10.3/§11.2 cover: why AC and MC are U-shaped (increasing → diminishing marginal returns), MC = AC at minimum AC, MC reflects variable costs only. Two additions here:
- ⚠️ **Terminology convention for this reading:** "**AC**" means average **TOTAL** cost (what M1 calls ATC). The curriculum states this explicitly.
- **Why MC eventually rises — wage example:** near full employment, extra workers require **higher wages** (overtime, higher offers) → marginal labor costs more → MC rises.

### Short-run equilibrium (Exhibit 7) 🔴 the capstone diagram
The firm **can earn an economic profit or loss** in the short run. Mechanics with P = 5.71 above minimum AC:

1. **Output where MR = MC** → point **A** (MC crosses the price line) → Q꜀
2. **TR** = P × Q꜀ (rectangle to the price line)
3. **TC** = Q꜀ × **AC at Q꜀** (point **B**, on AC directly below A)
4. **Economic profit = (P − AC) × Q꜀**

```
Economic profit = (P − AC) × Q,   evaluated at the Q where MR = MC
```

⚠️ **The A-vs-B trap:** output comes from **MC** (point A, MR = MC); AC (point B) is only used **afterwards to measure** profit. Picking output where P = AC or at min AC walks into the distractors. 🔗 M1 §11.4 (min ATC ≠ profit max), now drawn on a graph.

## 9e. Long-Run Equilibrium in Perfect Competition
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — "zero economic profit in the long run" is one of the most quoted conclusions in Level I Economics. Know the MECHANISM.**

### The adjustment mechanism (Exhibit 8) 🔴

> **⭐ YOU MISSED Q6 HERE.** *"If companies earn economic profits in a perfectly competitive market, over the long run the supply curve will most likely: shift to the RIGHT."* Two forces: **new entrants** + **existing firms expand capacity**. RIGHT = more supply = price falls. A LEFT shift is the mirror image — the response to economic **losses**. Re-read until automatic.

```
Short-run economic profit
   → attracts entrants (and incumbents expand)
   → aggregate supply ↑ → supply curve shifts RIGHT
   → given demand, PRICE FALLS   (5.71 → 4.50 in the exhibit)
```

⚠️ **Directional subtlety:** entry makes **industry** quantity rise (39 → 45) while **each incumbent's** quantity falls (Brown 10 → 8; Lopez 7 → 6). "Each firm produces more in the new equilibrium" is a trap.

### The long-run resting point (Exhibit 9) 🔴
Entry continues **as long as economic profit exists**, so it stops exactly at:
```
P = MC = minimum AC        (e.g., price 4.50 at output Qᴇ)
```
- **P = MC** → the MR = MC condition (MR = P for the price taker)
- **P = min AC** → TR = TC → **zero economic profit**

### Zero economic profit ≠ earning nothing ⚠️ 🔴 the distractor that never dies
The firm still earns its **normal profit** (rental cost of capital) — it's **inside economic costs**. 🔗 M1 §11.1: normal profit is a fixed cost. Closes the loop with §2.1 and §9d.

> **Complete one-liner:** *with low entry costs and homogeneous products, the perfectly competitive firm earns zero economic profit in the long run — but a full normal return.*

### Two structural facts 🔴
1. **The firm's supply curve = its (long-run) marginal cost schedule.** (Price taker: for any market price, quantity is read off MC via MR = MC — that mapping *is* a supply curve.)
2. **The firm's demand curve = the market equilibrium price** (horizontal = AR = MR).

### The firm's only decision 🟡
**How much to produce** (MR = MC). It can't set price, can't differentiate, can't advertise. Its one long-run lever: **relentlessly lowering cost** — the only route to (temporary) economic profit.

### Short run vs. long run side by side 🔴
| | Short run (Exh. 7) | Long run (Exh. 9) |
|---|---|---|
| Price vs. AC | P above or below min AC | **P = minimum AC** |
| Economic profit | Positive or negative | **Zero** |
| Firm earns | Econ profit/loss + normal return | **Normal profit only** |
| Why | Entry/exit still adjusting | Entry stops when profit gone |

## 9f. Monopoly: Demand, Supply, and Optimal Price/Output
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the MR = MC calculation and the "twice-as-steep" rule are among the most reliable calculations in the reading; the regulated-monopoly diagram is a guaranteed concept question.**

> **Two distinguishing characteristics:** (1) a **single firm IS the market**, (2) **significant barriers to entry**. The market demand function **is** the firm's demand schedule.

### Five characteristics (Exhibit 1 row expanded)
Single seller | no close substitute | entry very difficult | considerable pricing power | non-price differentiation via **advertising**.

### Sources of monopoly power 🔴 — all five, with examples
| Source | Mechanism | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Patents & copyrights** | Legal entry prevention; rewards R&D | — |
| **Control of critical resources** | Own the inputs | **De Beers** (⚠️ technically a **near-monopoly dominant firm**) |
| **Government authorization** = **natural monopoly** | Big fixed investment + **economies of scale** → one seller is cheaper | **Water/sewer, electricity** |
| **Brand loyalty** | Customers see no substitute | **Rolex** (monopoly-*like* power) |
| **Network effects** | Increasing returns to adoption | **Microsoft Word** (new hires need no training) |

⚠️ **Most common form = government-controlled authorization (natural monopoly)** — NOT patents. Likely distractor.

**Natural monopoly logic to quote:** significant capital investment + **declining long-run average costs** → one provider is **beneficial to society** (one water network beats dozens of parallel pipes). **Regulators set prices to allow a normal return — yet monopolists still attempt to maximize profits.**

### Revenue functions 🔴 CALCULATION CENTRAL
```
Demand:   QD = a − bP        →    P = a/b − (1/b)Q
TR = P × Q = (a/b)Q − (1/b)Q²
MR = (a/b) − (2/b)Q
AR = TR/Q = the demand function itself
```

🔴 **Twice-as-steep rule:** linear demand → **MR has the SAME intercept, TWICE the slope** (hits the Q-axis at half the demand curve's intercept). Why: to sell more, the monopolist **cuts price on ALL units** (🔗 M1 §10.2 decomposition MR = P + Q·ΔP/ΔQ).

**The natural gas example (used by all the exhibits):**
```
QD = 400 − 0.5P   →   P = 800 − 2Q
TR = 800Q − 2Q²
MR = 800 − 4Q          [same intercept 800, slope doubled −2 → −4]
AR = 800 − 2Q          [= demand ✓]
```

### The monopolist has NO supply curve ⚠️ 🔴
True for **monopoly, monopolistic competition, AND oligopoly**: no well-defined supply function — output comes from **MR = MC**, not a price→quantity schedule. **Only the perfectly competitive firm has a true supply curve (its MC).** Clean exam point.

**Cost side of the example:**
```
TC = 20,000 + 50Q + 3Q²    →    MC = 50 + 6Q
```
(Pattern: TC = F + cQ + dQ² → MC = c + 2dQ; fixed cost vanishes — MC is variable-only, 🔗 M1 §11.2.)

### THE core monopoly calculation 🔴 four steps
**Step 1 — Q\*: set MR = MC**
```
800 − 4Q = 50 + 6Q  →  750 = 10Q  →  Q* = 75
```
**Step 2 — P\*: plug Q\* into the DEMAND curve (never MR!)**
```
P* = 800 − 2(75) = 650
```
**Step 3 — Profit = TR − TC**
```
π = (800Q − 2Q²) − (20,000 + 50Q + 3Q²) = −20,000 + 750Q − 5Q²
π(75) = −20,000 + 56,250 − 28,125 = 8,125
```
**Step 4 — Graphically (Exhibit 11):** π = (Pᴇ − ACᴇ) × Qᴇ — the gap between the TR and TC rectangles.

⚠️ **The #1 monopoly trap: price comes from the DEMAND curve at Q\*, never from the MR = MC intersection.** The crossing gives *quantity*; travel **vertically up to demand** for price. The intersection-height price will be an answer choice — wrong.

*Alternative methods:* quadratic formula on π, or set Δπ/ΔQ = 0 — same Q* = 75.

### Always on the ELASTIC portion 🔴
MR = MC must occur where **MR > 0** (MC is never negative) → demand is elastic there. (Delivers §9b's promise.)

### The elasticity pricing rule 🔴 NEW CALCULATION
From MR = P[1 − 1/εp] and MR = MC:
```
P × [1 − (1/εp)] = MC
```
Price can be set knowing only **cost structure** and **elasticity**.

**Worked example:** MC = 75, εp = 1.5:
```
P × (1 − 1/1.5) = 75  →  P × (1/3) = 75  →  P = 225
```
(1 − 0.667 = 0.333; then **divide**, don't multiply. εp near 1 → markup explodes; εp → ∞ → P → MC, the competitive result.)

### Regulating the natural monopoly (Exhibit 12) 🔴 GUARANTEED concept material

> **⭐ YOU MISSED Q7 HERE.** *"A regulator of an authorized monopoly will most likely base regulated prices on: LONG-RUN AVERAGE COST."* P = LRAC is the price at which investors earn exactly a **normal return**. The distractors are the other two rows: **marginal cost** = the competitive solution (fails — losses under declining LRAC), **first-degree price discrimination** = the unregulated monopolist's dream. Learn all three rows as a set.

Setup: government franchise, **declining LRAC** (economies of scale), LRMC below LRAC. Three solutions, highest price → lowest:

| Solution | Rule | Output/Price | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Unregulated monopoly** | LRMC = MR, price off demand | **Qᴍ, Pᴍ** (lowest Q, highest P) | Best for firm, worst for consumers |
| **2. Competitive** | **P = MC** | **Q꜀, P꜀** (highest Q, lowest P) | ⚠️ With declining LRAC, **P꜀ < AC → losses**; needs a **subsidy** |
| **3. Regulatory** | **P = LRAC** (LRAC crosses demand) | **Qᴿ, Pᴿ** — in between | **Fair/normal return**, zero economic profit, no subsidy |

**Memory anchor:** **Pᴍ > Pᴿ > P꜀** and **Qᴍ < Qᴿ < Q꜀** — the regulator lands in the middle.

⚠️ **Why P = MC fails here (subtle, testable):** continuously declining AC means **MC < AC everywhere** in the relevant range → marginal-cost pricing guarantees losses. That is precisely why average-cost (regulatory) pricing exists.

## 9g. Price Discrimination: Extracting Consumer Surplus
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — classify a business practice as 1st/2nd/3rd degree.**

### The spectrum of monopoly effectiveness
| Extreme | Behavior | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Least effective** | Prices like perfect competition | **Regulation** or **threat of entry** |
| **Most effective** | **Extracts the entire consumer surplus** | First-degree discrimination |

🔗 §9c's triangle is the target — discrimination converts consumer surplus into producer revenue.

### The three degrees 🔴
| Degree | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | Charge **each customer** their **maximum willingness to pay** (needs each customer's demand schedule; helped by no public price disclosure) | Captures the **entire consumer surplus** |
| **Second** | **Menu of quantity/quality options** → customers **self-select** | Volume discounts/surcharges, coupons, bundling, "professional grade" tiers |
| **Third** | **Seller sorts customers** by observable trait/segment | Student vs. professional software; same-day airline roundtrips priced higher (business travelers) |

**Telling 2nd from 3rd (the usual difficulty):**
- **2nd** → the *customer* reveals type by **choosing** from a menu
- **3rd** → the *seller* sorts by an **observable trait** before quoting

**Memory hook:** 1st = **each person**; 2nd = **self-select**; 3rd = **sorted by segment**.

### The surprising welfare point ⚠️ 🔴
Under first-degree discrimination, **NOT every consumer is worse off**: some pay **below** the single monopoly price — served as long as **MR > MC**. Output expands toward the competitive quantity; what changes is **who keeps the surplus**. "All consumers are worse off" = **false**.

### Two-part tariff 🟡 — the café lunch club
Fixed monthly fee + half-price lunches:
1. Fee set at the **maximum you'd pay** for the half-price option → the **income effect is extracted** as the fee
2. Lower per-lunch price → you **buy more lunches** (downward-sloping demand)
3. Yet you're **no better or worse off** — the surplus left as the membership fee

**One line:** *the per-unit discount works the quantity margin; the fixed fee harvests the surplus.* (Sellers exploiting income + substitution effects — 🔗 M1 §7.)

## 9h. Monopoly: Long-Run Equilibrium
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — the headline contrast with perfect competition is a guaranteed comparison question.**

### The headline 🔴
> **The UNREGULATED monopoly can earn economic profit in the LONG RUN.**

| | Perfect competition | Unregulated monopoly |
|---|---|---|
| Long-run economic profit | **Zero** | **Can persist** |
| Why | Low barriers → entry until gone | **Substantial ONGOING barriers** block the mechanism |

### Long run vs. short run restated 🟡
- **Short run:** some factors fixed — generally **capital** (plant, machinery, technology, arable land)
- **Long run:** **ALL factors variable — including technology** ⚠️ (distractors exploit the technology detail)

### The maintenance condition ⚠️ 🔴
Long-run monopoly profit requires **ongoing** barriers: a patent-based moat needs **continuously added new patents**. One expiring patent = the moat drains.

### Four long-run solutions for regulated (natural) monopolies 🔴 matching material
| # | Solution | Mechanism | Example / Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | **MC pricing + subsidy** | P = MC won't cover AC (Exh. 12) → **subsidize the gap** | **Amtrak** |
| **2** | **Nationalization** | Government ownership | Europe; **US avoids it**. ⚠️ Raising prices at state enterprises is **politically unpopular** even as costs rise |
| **3** | **Regulator** | **Pᴿ = LRAC** → normal return ⭐ *(your missed Q7)* | ⚠️ (a) with no competitors, **risk is LOWER** → fair return is lower; (b) hard to verify the **true LRAC** (firm overstates costs) |
| **4** | **Franchise bidding** | Competitive bidding targeting **price = LRAC** | Airport/rail-station retail, stadium concessions |

**Memory hook — "S-N-R-F":** **S**ubsidize, **N**ationalize, **R**egulate, **F**ranchise. Solutions 3 and 4 share the target price (LRAC); they differ in **who enforces it** — regulator vs. bidding.

## 10. Quick-Reference Sheet (Module 2 content only)

```
── FOUR STRUCTURES (decreasing competition) ──
Perfect comp:   many | homogeneous | very low barriers | NO pricing power
                | NO non-price competition | earns NORMAL profit only
Monop. comp:    many | DIFFERENTIATED | low barriers | some power
                | advertising + differentiation
Oligopoly:      FEW | homogeneous | HIGH barriers | rivals' RETALIATION
Monopoly:       ONE | unique, no close substitutes | very high barriers
                | usually REGULATED to a normal return

Five factors:   Number | Differentiation | Pricing power | Barriers | Non-price
Porter's five:  entry threat | supplier power | buyer power
                | substitute threat | rivalry
Owners prefer monopoly/oligopoly; consumers prefer perfect competition
BUT imperfect competition can fund INNOVATION (clinical trials)

── PC: FIVE CONDITIONS ── (= the Exhibit 1 row, left to right)
many potential buyers AND sellers | identical products | low barriers
| no pricing power | no non-price competition
Benchmark only; canonical example: corn for animal feed

── DEMAND CURVES IN PC ──
MARKET: downward | FIRM: horizontal at market price → MR = P
"If one firm could move the market price → NOT perfect competition"

── NEW ELASTICITY LAYER ── (basics: M1 §4–6; εp = POSITIVE magnitude here)
Competition test: more/closer substitutes → LOWER pricing power
TR maximized where MR = 0 (= midpoint of linear demand = unit elastic)
MR = P[1 − (1/εp)]
  εp→∞: MR=P | εp>1: MR>0 | εp=1: MR=0 | εp<1: MR<0
Never operate on the inelastic portion

── CONSUMER SURPLUS ──
Demand curve = MARGINAL VALUE curve (willingness to pay per extra unit)
Total value = whole area under demand to Q₁
Expenditure = P₁ × Q₁ (rectangle)
CS = value − expenditure (triangle above price line)
Linear:  CS = ½ × Q₁ × (Pmax − P₁),  Pmax = PRICE intercept
Last unit's marginal value = P₁ exactly (zero surplus on it)

── PC: COSTS, EQUILIBRIUM, PROFIT ──
Market supply = SUM of firm supply curves
Equilibrium: inverse form (P=...) → equate P; direct form (Q=...) → equate Q
   e.g., 25 − 0.5Q = −2 + 0.2Q → Q* = 38.57, P* = 5.71
Economic profit = TR − TC (TC incl. normal return on ALL resources)
  = Accounting profit − unrecorded opportunity costs
"AC" in this reading = average TOTAL cost
Price taker: P = AR = MR (one horizontal line)
SHORT RUN: 1) Q at MR = MC (point A on MC)  2) read AC (point B)
           3) π = (P − AC) × Q      [A gives Q; B only measures π]

── PC: LONG RUN ──
Profit → entry + capacity expansion → supply RIGHT → price falls
(industry Q ↑ but each incumbent's Q ↓)
Resting point: P = MC = min AC → TR = TC → ZERO economic profit
              → still earns NORMAL profit (inside economic costs)
Firm's supply curve = its MC schedule (ONLY in perfect competition)

── MONOPOLY ──
Two traits: single firm = market; big entry barriers
Power sources: patents | critical resources (De Beers) | govt authorization
  = NATURAL monopoly (MOST COMMON; utilities) | brand (Rolex)
  | network effects (MS Word)
Linear demand P = a/b − (1/b)Q → MR: SAME intercept, TWICE the slope
AR = demand function (every structure)
NO supply curve (also monop. comp. & oligopoly)

PROFIT MAX (4 steps):
1. MR = MC:        800 − 4Q = 50 + 6Q → Q* = 75
2. P off DEMAND:   P* = 800 − 2(75) = 650   (NEVER off MR)
3. π = TR − TC:    −20,000 + 750(75) − 5(75²) = 8,125
4. Graph: π = (Pᴇ − ACᴇ) × Qᴇ
Always on the ELASTIC portion (MR = MC where MR > 0)
Elasticity pricing: P[1 − 1/εp] = MC
   e.g., MC=75, εp=1.5 → P(1/3)=75 → P=225

NATURAL MONOPOLY (Exh. 12):  Pᴍ > Pᴿ > P꜀  and  Qᴍ < Qᴿ < Q꜀
  M: LRMC=MR, price off demand | R: P=LRAC (fair return)
  | C: P=MC → LOSSES (MC<AC under declining LRAC) → needs subsidy

── PRICE DISCRIMINATION ──
1st: each pays their max → captures ENTIRE consumer surplus
     (not all worse off: some pay < monopoly price, served while MR > MC)
2nd: quantity/quality menu → SELF-SELECT
3rd: seller SORTS by observable segment
Two-part tariff: unit discount + fixed fee harvests the surplus

── MONOPOLY LONG RUN ──
UNREGULATED monopoly CAN keep economic profit (vs. PC: zero)
  — needs ONGOING barriers (patents must keep being added)
Long run = ALL factors variable, INCLUDING technology
Regulated solutions "S-N-R-F": Subsidize (P=MC, Amtrak) | Nationalize
  (Europe; price rises unpopular) | Regulate (P=LRAC; risk LOWER,
  LRAC hard to verify) | Franchise (bidding to P=LRAC; airports, stadiums)
```
