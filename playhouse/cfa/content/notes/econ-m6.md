## 0. Introduction — Framing Only
**Exam importance: 🟢 Low (no direct questions), but it names the reading's structure**

- **The investor's two questions:** *where* to invest and *in what asset classes*. Some countries attract on an **equity** view (strong growth, profitable sectors); others on a **fixed income** view (interest-rate environment, price stability). 🔗 M3 §10: the same equity-growth / fixed-income-inflation split.
- **Short-term cross-country factors:** expected GDP growth, monetary and fiscal policy, trade policy, competitiveness. **Long-term factors:** stage of economic and financial-market development, demographics, quantity and quality of physical and **human capital** (accumulated education and training), and **areas of comparative advantage**. (🔗 M3 §12 LRAS shifters — the same list, now used for country selection.)
- **How trade helps growth:** more efficient resource allocation, access to larger capital and product markets, **specialization based on comparative advantage**.
- **How capital flows help:** funds move from countries with **excess savings** to those where capital is **scarce** → more liquidity, higher output, **lower cost of capital**.
- **Roadmap:** terminology → patterns and trends → benefits of trade → **absolute vs. comparative advantage + two models of comparative advantage** → the **balance of payments** → key points and practice.

## 1. International Trade: Basic Terminology (§6.02)
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — GDP vs. GNP and terms of trade are reliable one-word-definition items; the trade surplus ↔ lending direction is a polarity item**

### GDP vs. GNP 🔴 — the "where vs. who" distinction
| Measure | Counts production by | Includes | Excludes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GDP** | Factors of production **located within** the country | Production by **foreigners inside** the country | Production by **citizens outside** the country |
| **GNP** | Factors **supplied by citizens**, wherever located | Production by **citizens abroad** | Production by **foreigners inside** the country |

**Memory hook:** GDP = **D**omestic = *where* (geography). GNP = **N**ational = *who* (nationality). A Korean-owned plant in Vietnam counts in Vietnam's GDP and Korea's GNP. ⚠️ Distractors swap the two inclusion rules — fix which one you're asked about before reading options.

### Imports, exports, net exports 🔴
- **Imports** = goods and services the domestic economy (households, firms, government) **purchases from** other countries.
- **Exports** = goods and services the domestic economy **sells to** other countries.
- **Net exports** = **exports − imports**.

| Net exports | State | Financial counterpart (polarity 🔴) |
|---|---|---|
| X = M | **Balanced trade** | — |
| X > M | **Trade surplus** | Country **lends to foreigners / buys foreign assets** — it's financing other countries' deficits |
| X < M | **Trade deficit** | Country **borrows from foreigners / sells assets to foreigners** |

⚠️ **The direction to fix:** a surplus country is a **net lender** (capital flows *out*); a deficit country is a **net borrower** (capital flows *in*). 🔗 M3 §6 ("a trade deficit must be financed by borrowing from the rest of the world") and M3 §7 (S − I − (G − T) = X − M). This is the identity the balance-of-payments section will formalize.

### Terms of trade 🔴 FORMULA + direction
```
Terms of trade  =  Price of exports / Price of imports
                   (measured with export and import price INDEXES)
```
- Captures the **relative cost of imports in terms of exports** — how many imports a unit of exports buys.
- **Export prices rise relative to import prices → terms of trade IMPROVE** — the country can buy **more imports with the same exports**.
- ⚠️ Polarity: an *improvement* is the ratio **rising**. A commodity exporter sees its terms of trade improve when commodity prices rise and deteriorate when they fall. Distractors invert the ratio or the direction.

### Autarky, open economies, and the price vocabulary 🟡
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Autarky** | A country does **not trade** — all goods produced *and* consumed domestically |
| **Autarkic price** | The price of a good in an autarkic economy |
| **Closed economy** | = autarkic economy (no trade) |
| **Open economy** | Trades with other countries |
| **World price** | The price prevailing in the world market — what open-economy members buy and sell at **if there are no trade restrictions** |
| **Free trade** | **No government restrictions** on the country's ability to trade → **global** aggregate demand and supply set the equilibrium quantity and price of imports and exports |
| **Trade protection** | Government policies restricting trade (**tariffs, quotas**, etc.) → prevent market forces from setting import/export price and quantity |

⚠️ Autarkic price vs. world price is the pair the trade-gains diagram will use: a country imports a good when its autarkic price is **above** the world price, exports when it's **below**. Keep the two prices separate.

### What an open economy offers 🟡 (the "benefits" list, first pass)
- Households: **larger variety** of goods and services, **more competitively priced**.
- Firms: access to **global markets and customers**; **capital-intensive industries** (automobiles, aircraft) exploit **economies of scale** from the larger market (🔗 M2 §9f natural-monopoly logic — scale economies).
- Investors: access to **foreign capital markets, foreign assets, more investment opportunities**.

## 2. Patterns and Trends in International Trade and Capital Flows (§6.03)
**Exam importance: 🟡 Medium overall — mostly descriptive data. The one 🔴 item is the FDI vs. FPI distinction; the rest is "know the direction of the trend" and the trade-growth link with its caveats.**

### The big-picture trend 🟡
- **Trade as a share of GDP rose in every region** over 1970–2006 (Exhibit 1). Fastest: **developing Asia**, from under 20% of GDP (1970) to over 70% (2006).
- World trade/GDP (Exhibit 2): 75.4% (1980s) → 77.9% → 91.6% → **94.1%** (2010–16). Gross trade (X + M) can exceed GDP — 🔗 M4 §3 (non-OECD high-income: **138.8%**).
- 2010–2016: a **decline** in both trade share and growth consistent with the global downturn, but **varied by country group**.

### Trade openness and growth — Exhibit 2 reading 🟡
| Group | Growth 2000–06 / 2000–09 | Growth 2010–16 |
|---|---|---|
| High-income OECD | 2.4% (2000–06) | **1.1%** |
| High-income non-OECD | 5.0% (2000–06) | **2.0%** |
| Lower-middle income | 7.7% (2000–06) | **3.3%** |

- Pattern: openness and growth rose together in most regions 1990–2009; growth slowed everywhere after 2010.
- **World Bank 2009:** "all rich and emerging economies are oriented to being open to trade." Mechanisms it names: when exports concentrate in **labor-intensive manufacturing**, trade **raises wages for unskilled workers** (helps the poor); encourages **macroeconomic stability** (the poor are hurt most by inflation); enhances **productivity through innovation and factor accumulation**.
- ⚠️ **Two caveats the exam can quote:** (1) "there may be some **empirical uncertainty** about the strength of trade's relationship with growth"; (2) **trade is not the only growth factor** — quality of **institutions, infrastructure, education; economic systems; degree of development; global market conditions** (WTO 2008). An option claiming trade *determines* growth overstates it.

### FDI vs. FPI 🔴 — the definition pair
| | **Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)** | **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI)** |
|---|---|---|
| What | Direct investment by a **firm** in one country (**source**) in **productive assets** in another (**host**) | **Shorter-term** investment in **foreign financial instruments** — foreign stocks, foreign government bonds |
| Who | Firms → become **multinational corporations (MNCs)**: operating in, or with subsidiaries in, more than one country | **Individuals, firms, institutional investors** (e.g., pension funds) |
| Horizon | Long-term, control-oriented | Shorter-term, financial |

**Memory hook:** **D**irect = **productive assets** (a factory); **P**ortfolio = **paper** (securities). ⚠️ The distractor swaps the asset type. Also: an FDI firm *is* an MNC by definition.

### Exhibit 3 — selected country facts 🟢
- World trade/GDP: **39% (1990) → 57% (2010)**.
- **Argentina** 15% → 35%; **India** 16% → ~50%; **Germany** 46% → **87%** (1990–2017, sharp); **United States** 20% → 27% (modest).
- **FDI inflows and outflows rose** as a share of GDP for every country shown between 1990 and 2000.

### Why MNCs matter — intra-firm trade and the global value chain 🟡
- **Intra-firm trade** (between MNCs and their subsidiaries) is a major part of world trade — **46% of US imports** occur between **related parties** (Bernard, Jensen, Redding & Schott 2010).
- **Globalization of production:** firms **decompose the value chain** into components and **outsource each to where it's produced most efficiently** → higher productive efficiency. Example: the 2016 iPhone 6s — camera/display/storage (Japan), RAM/A9 (South Korea), modem/battery/Wi-Fi/chassis (China), design (US), assembly (China). Intel: 10 fabs and 101 assembly/test sites in 8 countries.
- **FDI and outsourcing** raise business investment in host economies and let **smaller, less developed economies participate in trade**.
- **The cost of interdependence:** economies are **more exposed to global competition** → must be **more flexible in production structure** to respond to changes in global demand and supply. (🔗 M4 §3: the external sector's cyclical transmission.)

## 3. Gains from Trade: Absolute and Comparative Advantage (§6.04)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — THE guaranteed calculation of this reading. Every exam has an output-per-worker table and asks who has comparative advantage in what. This is simultaneously a wrong-quantity trap (the ratio flips between the two goods) and a polarity trap (lower opportunity cost wins, not higher).**

### The two definitions 🔴
| Concept | Definition | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Absolute advantage** | Can produce the good at **lower cost / with fewer resources** than the trading partner | **Higher output per worker** (read the table directly) |
| **Comparative advantage** | **Lower opportunity cost** of producing the good than the trading partner | **Compute the ratio** — what you give up of the *other* good |

### The opportunity-cost calculation 🔴 — fixed routine
```
Opportunity cost of 1 unit of good A  =  (output of B) / (output of A)
                                      =  units of B given up per unit of A
```
**The ratio for good B is the reciprocal.** Comparative advantage in A goes to the country with the **LOWER** number.

**Curriculum example 1 — pens and pencils (verified):**
| | Pens/day | Pencils/day | OC of 1 pen (in pencils) | OC of 1 pencil (in pens) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Brazil | 20 | 40 | 40/20 = **2** | 20/40 = **1/2** |
| Vietnam | 10 | 60 | 60/10 = **6** | 10/60 = **1/6** |

- Absolute: Brazil in pens (20 > 10); Vietnam in pencils (60 > 40).
- Comparative: **Brazil in pens** (2 < 6); **Vietnam in pencils** (1/6 < 1/2).

**Example 1 (Exhibit 4) — UK and India (verified):**
| | Machinery | Cloth (yards) | OC of 1 machine (in cloth) | OC of 1 yard cloth (in machines) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| United Kingdom | 4 | 8 | 8/4 = **2** | 4/8 = **1/2** |
| India | 2 | 16 | 16/2 = **8** | 2/16 = **1/8** |

- **1A/1B absolute:** UK in machinery (4 > 2); India in cloth (16 > 8).
- **2A/2B comparative:** UK in machinery (2 < 8); India in cloth (1/8 < 1/2). Both "yes" — here absolute and comparative line up. They don't always.
- The OC of a machine in the UK (2 yards) **is the UK's autarkic price of machines in terms of cloth** — the no-trade relative price. 🔗 §1 autarkic price.

⚠️ **The three traps, in order of frequency:**
1. **Wrong direction of the ratio.** OC of a machine = cloth/machinery, *not* machinery/cloth. Anchor: *"to make one more machine, how much cloth do I give up?"* — the thing given up goes on top.
2. **Picking the higher number.** Comparative advantage = **lower** opportunity cost. India's 8 is the *worst* machine-maker, not the best.
3. **Reading absolute as comparative.** A country can have absolute advantage in *both* goods and still only comparative advantage in *one* — see below.

### Comparative advantage without absolute advantage 🔴 — the point of the theory
Suppose India could make only **6 yards of cloth** (not 16). Then the UK has absolute advantage in **both** goods (4 > 2 machines, 8 > 6 cloth). But:
```
OC of 1 yard cloth: India = 2/6 = 1/3 machine   <   UK = 1/2 machine
```
India **still has comparative advantage in cloth** and still gains by exporting it.
> **One-liner:** *a country with no absolute advantage in anything still gains from trade by exporting what it has comparative advantage in.* An option saying a country with no absolute advantage cannot gain from trade is **false**.

### Gains from trade and the range of world prices 🔴
- Both countries gain if the world price lies **between the two autarkic prices**: here, between **2 and 8 yards of cloth per machine** (UK sells a machine for *more than 2*; India buys one for *less than 8*).
- The **exact** world price needs demand and supply details — the exam only asks for the **range**.

**Who gains more — the distance rule 🔴:**
> *"The further away the world price is from a country's autarkic price, the more that country gains from trade."*

| World price (cloth per machine) | UK's gain per machine sold (vs. autarkic 2) | Who it favors |
|---|---|---|
| **7** (close to India's 8) | 7 − 2 = **5 yards** | **UK** |
| **3** (close to UK's 2) | 3 − 2 = **1 yard** | India |

⚠️ Polarity: the country whose autarkic price the world price sits **far from** gains most; the one it sits **near** gains little. A price close to *your own* autarkic price means you're barely better off than not trading.

### Comparative advantage changes over time 🟡
Drivers: structural shifts in the domestic economy, shifts in the global economy, **accumulation of physical or human capital**, **new technology**, **discovery of natural resources** (oil). Example: rising skilled labor in China → MNCs locate **R&D facilities** there.

**Analyst use:** examine a country's comparative and absolute advantages *and changes in them*; watch policy/regulation, demographics, human capital, demand conditions → identify sectors → industries → companies that benefit.

## 4. Ricardian and Heckscher–Ohlin Models (§6.05)
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — "which model, which source of advantage" is a near-guaranteed one-liner. The models differ on exactly two things (number of factors; what drives advantage), and the exam swaps them. Also the source of the "trade redistributes income" idea.**

### The comparison table 🔴 — memorize both columns
| | **Ricardian model** | **Heckscher–Ohlin (H–O) model** |
|---|---|---|
| Also called | — | **Factor-proportions theory** |
| Variable factor(s) of production | **Labor only** | **Capital AND labor** (each good can be made with varying combinations) |
| **Source of comparative advantage** | **Differences in labor productivity → reflecting differences in TECHNOLOGY** | **Differences in relative FACTOR ENDOWMENTS** (capital-to-labor ratio) |
| Technology assumption | Differs across countries (that's the driver) | **Same across countries within an industry; differs between industries** |
| Who exports what | The good with the **lower opportunity cost** | The good **intensive in the country's relatively abundant factor** |
| Income redistribution? | Not addressed (one factor) | **Yes** — two factors → trade shifts income between them |

**Memory hook:** **R**icardo = **R**elative productivity (technology, one factor). **H–O** = **H**ow much of each factor you **O**wn (endowments, two factors).

### Ricardian model — details 🟡
- **Adam Smith:** gains from trade need **absolute** advantage. **David Ricardo** extended it: a country with **no absolute advantage in anything** still gains via **comparative** advantage (🔗 §3's India-with-6-cloth case).
- Lower opportunity cost → comparative advantage → **specialize** in that good.
- **Size matters for specialization:** in a two-country model, the **smaller country may specialize completely** but can't meet total demand; the **larger country is incompletely specialized** — it exports its comparative-advantage good but still produces (and consumes) some of the other.
- **Advantage can shift:** technology gaps close or reverse. **IT services → India** is the curriculum's example, driven by a growing pool of skilled, relatively low-wage labor, telecom infrastructure growth, and 1990s trade liberalization.

### Heckscher–Ohlin — details 🔴
- **Relative abundance test:** a country is **capital-abundant** if its **capital/labor endowment ratio exceeds** its trading partner's (and labor-abundant if lower). It's the **ratio**, not the absolute amount.
- **Prediction:** a labor-abundant country **exports labor-intensive goods and imports capital-intensive goods.** Textiles and clothing are labor-intensive → exported by **China and India**.
- **Factor intensity example (2002):** Canadian **paper** industry capital per worker **C$118,777** vs. **clothing** **C$8,954** → paper is capital-intensive. Canada (capital-abundant vs. Thailand) **exports paper to Thailand, imports clothing** from it.

### Trade redistributes income — the H–O implication 🔴
Because H–O has **two factors**, it allows for **income redistribution through trade**:
```
Country opens to trade
  → price of the EXPORT good ↑, price of the IMPORT good ↓
  → demand for the factors used in each changes (DERIVED demand — demand for an
    input is derived from demand for the product it makes)
  → ABUNDANT factor gains (its good's price rose → more demand for it)
  → SCARCE factor loses
```
⚠️ **Polarity item:** opening to trade is **favorable to the abundant factor, negative for the scarce factor.** A labor-abundant country's workers gain; its capital owners lose. Distractors flip it. 🔗 M1 §9.1: derived demand for factors.

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED OFFICIAL Q6 HERE
> *"According to the H–O model, when trade opens:"* — you picked **"the scarce factor gains relative to the abundant factor"**; correct is **the abundant factor gains**.
> The chain is three links and the first one decides it: **the export good uses the abundant factor intensively** → trade raises export output → derived demand for the abundant factor rises → it gains. "Scarce" sounds like it should command a premium — that's intuition, not the model. The curriculum adds a detail worth keeping: this redistribution happens **within each country**, not just between them (option C's lure). *(Polarity miss — build from "export good = abundant factor", never from who sounds scarce.)*

### Endowments change → trade patterns change 🟡
- **Japan 1967:** comparative advantage in **unskilled-labor-intensive** goods (textiles, apparel, leather). **By 1980** (Meier 1998): greatly increased **skilled labor** → advantage in **skill-intensive** products, especially **non-electrical machinery**. Endowment shifts create investment opportunities (🔗 §3 "comparative advantage changes over time").

### The models are complementary, not competing 🔴
- **Technology differences (Ricardian) and factor-abundance differences (H–O) are both important drivers of trade — complementary, not mutually exclusive.**
- **Tastes and preferences** also vary across countries and over time → affect trade patterns and flows.
- ⚠️ An option saying one model "replaces" or "disproves" the other is wrong. Both models rest on assumptions that "may not be fully satisfied in the real world" but give "extremely useful insights."

## 5. Balance of Payments: Accounts and Components (§6.06)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — "which account does this transaction belong in?" is a guaranteed classification item, and the BOP debit/credit convention is a polarity item that runs backwards from everyday intuition. This section is pure truncated-rule territory (sub-account lists) — memorize the lists in full.**

### What the BOP is 🟡
- A **double-entry bookkeeping system** summarizing a country's **economic transactions with the rest of the world** for a period (quarter or year).
- **Transaction** = "an economic flow that reflects the creation, transformation, exchange, or extinction of economic value" — changes in ownership of goods/financial assets, provision of services, provision of labour and capital.
- → BOP covers **payments for exports and imports + financial transactions + financial transfers.**
- Why analysts care: it feeds assessment of the macro environment, monetary/fiscal policy, long-term growth potential; investors use trade and capital-flow data to evaluate **capital investment, profitability, and risk**.

### Double entry — the accounting rule 🔴
- Every transaction = **a debit AND a credit**. In principle Σ debits = Σ credits → **net balance = zero**.
- ⚠️ **In practice it rarely nets to zero** — the data come from **different sources** (the statistical discrepancy).

### Debits vs. credits — the convention that runs backwards 🔴 POLARITY
| | **DEBIT** | **CREDIT** |
|---|---|---|
| Balance-sheet meaning | **Increase in assets** or **decrease in liabilities** | **Decrease in assets** or **increase in liabilities** |
| Examples (Exhibit 6, Panel A) | Value of **imported** goods and services · Purchases of **foreign financial assets** · Receipt of payments from foreigners · Increase in debt owed **by** foreigners · Payment of debt owed **to** foreigners | **Payments** for imports · Payments for foreign financial assets · Value of **exported** goods and services · Payment of debt **by** foreigners · Increase in debt owed **to** foreigners |

**The memory anchor:** think of it as the country's balance sheet. **Importing goods = acquiring an asset → DEBIT.** **Exporting goods = giving up an asset → CREDIT.** Owing money to foreigners = a liability going up → CREDIT. ⚠️ This is the opposite of "exports are good, so they're a credit = positive" intuition... except it lands in the same place: **exports ARE credits.** Don't reason from "good vs. bad"; reason from **asset ↑ = debit, liability ↑ = credit**.

### The worked entry — Exhibit 6, Panel B 🔴 trace it
**1 Sept:** Country A buys $1m of goods from Country B, pays 1 Dec.

| Date | Country A records | Country B records |
|---|---|---|
| **1 Sept** | **Debit** $1m — goods purchased (real assets ↑) / **Credit** $1m — short-term liability to B (financial liabilities ↑) | **Debit** $1m — short-term claim on A (financial assets ↑) / **Credit** $1m — goods delivered (real assets ↓) |
| **1 Dec** | **Debit** $1m — liability eliminated (financial liabilities ↓) / **Credit** $1m — payment made (financial assets ↓) | **Debit** $1m — cash received (financial assets ↑) / **Credit** $1m — claim eliminated (financial assets ↓) |

Each date, each country: one debit, one credit, equal amounts. The import itself is a **debit** for A; the export is a **credit** for B.

### The three accounts 🔴 — and their sub-accounts (memorize in full)
| Account | Measures |
|---|---|
| **Current account** | Flow of **goods and services** (+ income + transfers) |
| **Capital account** | **Transfers of capital** |
| **Financial account** | **Investment flows** |

#### Current account — four sub-accounts 🔴
1. **Merchandise trade** — all commodities and manufactured goods bought, sold, or given away.
2. **Services** — tourism, transportation, engineering, business services (legal, consulting, accounting); **fees from patents and copyrights** on technology, software, books, movies.
3. **Income receipts** — income from **ownership of assets**: dividends, interest. ⚠️ **Income on foreign investment is in the CURRENT account** (not financial) because it's compensation for services the investment provides — a German plant in China yields profits viewed as a **service export from Germany to China**. The *investment* goes in the financial account; the *income from it* goes here.
4. **Unilateral transfers** — one-way transfers: **worker remittances** home, **foreign aid, gifts**.

#### Capital account — two sub-accounts 🟡
1. **Capital transfers** — **debt forgiveness**; **migrants' transfers** (goods and financial assets migrants carry when they move); transfer of title to fixed assets; funds linked to sale/acquisition of fixed assets; gift and inheritance taxes; death duties; uninsured damage to fixed assets; legacies.
2. **Sales and purchases of non-produced, non-financial assets** — **rights to natural resources**; intangibles: **patents, copyrights, trademarks, franchises, leases**.

⚠️ Patent trap: **fees** from patents (licensing income) → **current account, services**. **Sale of the patent itself** → **capital account**. Flow vs. asset.

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED OFFICIAL Q8 HERE — the patent trap, as written above
> *"Patent fees and legal services are recorded in:"* — you picked **financial account**; correct is **current account** (services sub-account).
> Both items are **services** — a flow of value for work done, not an investment and not an asset sale. The financial account is for **investment flows** only (securities, direct investment, reserves). Decision routine for any BOP item: (1) is it a **good/service/income/transfer** → current; (2) is it the **sale of a non-produced asset or a capital transfer** → capital; (3) is it an **investment position changing** → financial. *(Truncated-rule miss — the rule was in the note two lines up; "patent" pulled toward "asset" without checking fee vs. sale.)* Official Q7 (mineral rights → capital ✓) shows the asset side was stored; the flow side wasn't.

#### Financial account — two sub-accounts 🔴
1. **The country's assets abroad** — split into **official reserve assets, government assets, private assets**: gold, foreign currencies, foreign securities, the government's **reserve position in the IMF**, **direct foreign investment**, claims reported by resident banks.
2. **Foreign-owned assets in the reporting country** — split into **official assets** and **other foreign assets**: securities issued by the government and private sector (bonds, equities, MBS), **direct investment**, foreign liabilities reported by the banking sector.

### Classification drill — where does it go? 🔴
| Transaction | Account (sub-account) |
|---|---|
| Export of cars | Current (merchandise trade) |
| A tourist's hotel bill abroad | Current (services) |
| Dividend received from a foreign stock | Current (income receipts) |
| Remittance sent home by a migrant worker | Current (unilateral transfers) |
| Forgiving a foreign government's debt | Capital (capital transfers) |
| Selling a trademark to a foreign firm | Capital (non-produced, non-financial assets) |
| Buying a foreign company's bonds | Financial (assets abroad — private) |
| A foreign firm building a plant domestically | Financial (foreign-owned assets — direct investment) |
| Central bank adding foreign currency reserves | Financial (assets abroad — official reserves) |

## 6. National Economic Accounts and the Balance of Payments (§6.07)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the current-account identity CA = Sₚ + S_g − I is the reading's second guaranteed calculation and its heaviest polarity item. "A current-account deficit must be offset by a capital + financial account surplus" is a near-certain one-liner. This section is M3 §7's identity with the signs re-labeled; the M3 polarity warning applies in full.**

### From the closed to the open economy 🔴
```
Closed economy:   Y = C + I + G                                 (Equation 1)
Open economy:     Y = C + I + G + X − M                         (Equation 2)
```
🔗 M3 §6 — same identity. New here: **net exports (X − M) = the current account balance** from a BOP perspective.

| Trade position | Current account | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| M > X | **Deficit** | Country is a **net international borrower** |
| X > M | **Surplus** | Country is a **net international lender** |

The current account **affects GDP and employment** (it's on the right side of Equation 2) and **measures the size and direction of international borrowing**.

### The BOP must balance — the offset rule 🔴
> **A current-account deficit or surplus must be offset by an opposite balance in the sum of the capital and financial accounts.**

- Current-account **deficit** → the country **increases its net foreign debts** by the deficit amount. **US example:** years of CA deficits financed by **net capital imports** — FDI by foreigners, loans from foreign banks, sales of US equities and bonds to foreign investors.
- Current-account **surplus** → the country **earns more on exports than it spends on imports** → it **finances its partners' deficits by lending to them** (bank loans, financial and real assets) → its **foreign wealth rises** because foreigners pay for imports by **issuing liabilities they must eventually redeem**. **Japan, Germany, China** = traditional surplus countries accumulating net foreign claims, especially against the US.

⚠️ **Polarity:** CA deficit ↔ **capital/financial account surplus** (foreign money flowing *in*). CA surplus ↔ capital/financial account **deficit** (domestic money flowing *out*). The two sides have opposite signs by construction. 🔗 §1 trade surplus = lender.

### The current account from the income side 🔴
```
CA  =  X − M  =  Y − (C + I + G)                               (Equation 3)
```
Read it: CA = **output minus domestic absorption**. **Consume more than you produce → CA deficit (borrow from foreigners); consume less → CA surplus (lend to foreigners).**

**Inter-temporal trade 🟡:** international capital flows are trade across *time*. A CA-deficit economy is **importing present consumption and exporting future consumption** (it will repay later). Definable term — expect it verbatim.

### The derivation — be able to reproduce it 🔴
```
Yᵈ = Y + R − T          disposable income = income + net transfers − taxes   (Eq. 4)
Yᵈ = C + Sₚ             disposable income is consumed or saved (private)     (Eq. 5)
C  = Y + R − T − Sₚ     combine 4 and 5                                       (Eq. 6)

Substitute Eq. 6 into Eq. 3 and rearrange:
CA = Sₚ − I + (T − G − R)                                                    (Eq. 7)

(T − G − R) = taxes − spending − transfers = GOVERNMENT SAVING, S_g

Sₚ + S_g = I + CA                                                            (Eq. 8)
```
🔗 M3 §7: this is S = I + (G − T) + (X − M) with S_g = −(G − T) and CA = X − M. Same identity, cleaner labels.

### Equation 8 — what an open economy can do that a closed one can't 🔴
- **Closed economy:** saving can **only** fund domestic investment (Sₚ + S_g = I).
- **Open economy:** saving funds **domestic investment OR foreign investment** (acquiring foreign assets = a CA surplus). And the reverse: a country with good investment opportunities is **not constrained by its domestic savings rate** — it can **raise I by increasing foreign borrowing (a reduction in CA) without raising domestic saving.**
- **India high-speed-rail example:** India imports all materials from France and borrows (perhaps from France) to pay. Domestic investment ↑ (the materials expand the capital stock); CA deficit ↑ by exactly the increase in I; **India's saving need not rise.** Inter-temporal: imports present consumption (borrows now), exports future consumption (repays later).

### Equation 9 — the three uses of private saving 🟡
```
Sₚ = I + CA − S_g                                                            (Eq. 9)
```
Private saving goes to: (1) **domestic capital investment** (I), (2) **purchases of assets from foreigners** (CA), (3) **net purchases of government debt** (−S_g — a government deficit absorbs private saving). (🔗 M3 §6: government competes with firms for household saving.)

### Equation 10 — the sources of a current-account imbalance 🔴 CALCULATION
```
CA = Sₚ + S_g − I                                                            (Eq. 10)
```
| CA **deficit** results from | CA **surplus** results from |
|---|---|
| **Low private saving**, **high investment**, **government deficit (S_g < 0)** — or a combination | **High private saving**, **low investment**, **government surplus** |

**Worked (verified):** Sₚ = 400, government deficit of 50 (S_g = −50), I = 380 → CA = 400 − 50 − 380 = **−30** (deficit). Sₚ = 300, T − G − R = +20, I = 250 → CA = **+70** (surplus).

⚠️ **The sign traps (your M3 §7 pattern):**
1. **Government deficit enters as NEGATIVE S_g.** "Fiscal deficit of 50" → S_g = −50, not +50.
2. **Investment is SUBTRACTED.** High I pushes CA *down*.
3. **CA = X − M**: a negative CA is a *deficit*. Don't flip the sign when converting to "trade deficit of…"
Substitute every sign explicitly — never from intuition.

### Is a current-account deficit "bad"? — the quality distinction 🔴
| If the deficit mainly reflects... | Capacity to repay later |
|---|---|
| **High private or government CONSUMPTION** (scarce saving = Sₚ + S_g) | **Unchanged** — nothing productive was added |
| **Strong INVESTMENT (I)** | **Improves** — productive resources expand, so the ability to repay rises |

> **One-liner:** *a deficit financing investment builds the means to repay; a deficit financing consumption does not.* The India rail case is the "good" kind.

> ### ⭐ YOU MISSED OFFICIAL Q10 HERE — "least alarming"
> *"Which chronic deficit condition is LEAST alarming to creditors?"* — you picked **high consumption**; correct is **high private investment**.
> Least alarming = the one that **improves** the ability to repay = investment. The quality table above says it directly; the stem's **least** inverted the question and you answered "which is alarming?". Third negation miss (M4 Ex7 Q1, M4 official Q14, now this). The T/F routine applies to *least* as much as to *NOT*: mark each option alarming / not alarming, then pick the odd one out.

### Deficits, interest rates, and the currency — the two horizons 🔴 POLARITY
```
SHORT RUN:  CA deficit ← strong domestic economy (high C, G, I)
              → elevated credit demand → HIGH interest rates
              → widening rate differentials vs. other countries
              → growing net capital IMPORTS
              → APPRECIATING currency

LONG RUN:   persistent CA deficit → permanent rise in foreign claims on the country
              → foreign investors demand RISING RISK PREMIUMS
              → DEPRECIATING currency
```
⚠️ **The horizon flips the currency direction.** Short run: deficit + high rates → appreciation (capital inflows chase the yield — 🔗 M3 §9 real-exchange-rate effect). Long run: deficit + mounting claims → depreciation (risk premium). An option that gives one direction without a horizon is incomplete; the stem will specify "in the long run" or describe "a persistent deficit."

## 7. Quick-Reference Sheet

```
── TERMINOLOGY (§6.02) ──
GDP = production WITHIN borders (incl. foreigners inside)
GNP = production BY CITIZENS (incl. citizens abroad)   D = where, N = who
Net exports = X − M;  surplus → LENDS to foreigners / buys foreign assets;
  deficit → BORROWS / sells assets to foreigners
Terms of trade = P_exports / P_imports (indexes); ratio ↑ = IMPROVED
Autarky = no trade (closed economy); autarkic price vs. WORLD price
Free trade = no govt restrictions; trade protection = tariffs, quotas

── PATTERNS (§6.03) ──
Trade/GDP rose everywhere 1970–2006; fastest developing Asia (<20% → >70%)
FDI = FIRM → PRODUCTIVE ASSETS abroad (source → host); firm = MNC
FPI = shorter-term, foreign FINANCIAL instruments (stocks, govt bonds)
Trade ↔ growth link: real but "empirical uncertainty"; not the only factor
  (institutions, infrastructure, education, systems, development, global)
Intra-firm trade = 46% of US imports; value chain decomposed & outsourced

── ABSOLUTE vs COMPARATIVE (§6.04) ── ⭐ THE CALC
Absolute = more output per worker (read the table)
Comparative = LOWER OPPORTUNITY COST
  OC of 1 unit of A = (output of B) / (output of A)  ← thing given up on TOP
  other good = reciprocal; LOWER number wins
UK 4 mach/8 cloth, India 2/16: OC machine UK 2, India 8 → UK machines;
  OC cloth UK 1/2, India 1/8 → India cloth
No absolute advantage anywhere → STILL gains via comparative advantage
Both gain if world price is BETWEEN autarkic prices (2–8 cloth/machine)
DISTANCE RULE: further the world price from YOUR autarkic price, the MORE
  you gain (price 7 → UK gains 5; price 3 → UK gains 1)
Advantage shifts: capital/human capital accumulation, technology, resources

── RICARDIAN vs H–O (§6.05) ──
Ricardian: LABOR only; advantage from TECHNOLOGY (productivity);
  smaller country specializes completely, larger incompletely
H–O (factor-proportions): CAPITAL + LABOR; advantage from FACTOR
  ENDOWMENTS (K/L RATIO vs partner); same tech across countries;
  export the good intensive in your ABUNDANT factor
H–O redistribution: export price ↑, import price ↓ → DERIVED demand →
  ABUNDANT factor GAINS, SCARCE factor LOSES
Complementary, not mutually exclusive; tastes matter too

── BOP ACCOUNTS (§6.06) ──
Double entry: every transaction = debit + credit; nets to ~0 (data sources)
DEBIT = asset ↑ or liability ↓ (IMPORTS, buying foreign assets, cash received)
CREDIT = asset ↓ or liability ↑ (EXPORTS, payments made, debt owed to foreigners ↑)
CURRENT (goods & services): merchandise | services (incl. patent FEES)
  | income receipts (dividends, interest — income on FOREIGN INVESTMENT
  is CURRENT) | unilateral transfers (remittances, aid, gifts)
CAPITAL (transfers of capital): capital transfers (debt forgiveness,
  migrants' transfers) | non-produced non-financial assets (resource
  rights, patents/trademarks SOLD)
FINANCIAL (investment flows): assets abroad (official reserves, govt,
  private — gold, FX, securities, IMF position, FDI) | foreign-owned
  assets at home (official, other — bonds, equities, direct investment)

── NATIONAL ACCOUNTS & BOP (§6.07) ── ⭐ THE IDENTITY
Y = C + I + G + X − M;  CA = X − M = Y − (C + I + G)
M > X → CA DEFICIT → net BORROWER;  X > M → SURPLUS → net LENDER
CA deficit MUST be offset by capital + financial account SURPLUS
  (US: deficits financed by FDI, bank loans, asset sales to foreigners;
   Japan/Germany/China: surpluses → net foreign claims)
Inter-temporal trade: deficit = import present consumption, export future
Derivation: Yᵈ = Y + R − T = C + Sₚ → CA = Sₚ − I + (T − G − R)
S_g = T − G − R (government saving)
Sₚ + S_g = I + CA         open economy: saving → domestic I OR foreign;
                          can raise I via foreign borrowing (CA ↓)
Sₚ = I + CA − S_g         three uses of private saving
CA = Sₚ + S_g − I         ⭐ govt DEFICIT = NEGATIVE S_g; I SUBTRACTED
  deficit ← low Sₚ, high I, govt deficit;  surplus ← the reverse
Deficit QUALITY: consumption-driven → repay capacity unchanged;
  investment-driven → repay capacity IMPROVES (India rail)
TWO HORIZONS: SR deficit + high rates → capital inflows → APPRECIATION;
  LR persistent deficit → rising risk premiums → DEPRECIATION
```
