## 0. The Introduction — Framing + Two Definitions
**Exam importance: 🟡 Medium — the two definitions ARE tested (Example 1), always against "tool" or "secondary use" distractors**

### Why governments matter more than households or firms 🟢
- A single household's consumption decision, or a single corporation's investment decision, has **negligible** impact on a large economy. Government decisions have **enormous** impact, for **two reasons**:
  1. The public sector **employs a significant proportion of the population** and is responsible for a **significant proportion of spending**.
  2. Governments are the **largest borrowers in world debt markets**.
- Government policy is ultimately expressed through its **borrowing and spending**.

### The two definitions 🔴 — memorize the exact scope
| Policy | Definition (curriculum wording) | Who | Lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monetary policy** | Central bank activities directed toward **influencing the quantity of money and credit** in an economy | **Central bank** | Money and credit |
| **Fiscal policy** | The government's decisions about **taxation and spending** | **Government** | Taxes and spending |

**Shared purpose:** both regulate economic activity over time — **accelerate** growth when the economy slows, **moderate** it when overheating. **Fiscal policy additionally** can **redistribute income and wealth**.

⚠️ **The distractor structure (Example 1 — you got both right):**
- *"Monetary policy involves setting medium-term targets for broad money aggregates"* — that's a **tool** of monetary policy, not what monetary policy *is*. The definition is about influencing the **macro economy** via money and credit.
- *"Monetary policy involves the central bank manipulating the government's budget deficit"* — wrong **actor**: the deficit is fiscal.
- *"Fiscal policy is used to redistribute wealth"* — a **secondary use**, not the overriding goal.
- *"Fiscal policy is the attempt to balance the budget year to year"* — a possible objective, not the definition.
> **Rule:** when a definition question offers a *tool*, a *use*, or an *objective* of the policy, those are distractors. The definition names the **actor** and the **lever**, and the purpose is **influencing broad economic activity**.

### The overarching goal 🟡
- Create an environment where **growth is stable and positive** and **inflation is stable and low**.
- The aim is to **steer the economy away from booms that are followed by extended low/negative growth and high unemployment** — i.e., to dampen the cycle (🔗 M4 §1 phase grid).
- Payoff: households feel secure in consumption/saving decisions; corporations can focus on sound investment, regular coupon payments, and profits.

### Why it's hard 🟡
1. Economies are buffeted by **shocks** (e.g., oil price jumps — 🔗 M3 §13 stagflation).
2. Some economists believe **natural cycles** exist regardless of policy.
3. History has plenty of cases where policy — monetary, fiscal, or both — **exacerbated an expansion** that ended in damage to the real economy, financial markets, and investors. (Policy can be pro-cyclical by mistake — 🔗 M3 §13's variable-lag warning.)

### Roadmap of the reading
The reading focuses on the relationship between **money, interest rates, and inflation**. Order: functions and role of **money** → the special role of **central banks** → how monetary and fiscal policy are implemented → their effects on interest rates and inflation.

## 1. Money: Functions, Creation, and Definition (§5.02)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — the money multiplier is a guaranteed calculation; the three functions and the narrow/broad distinction are reliable definition items**

### Why money exists — the barter economy's four problems 🟡
A **barter economy** = agents pay for goods and services with other goods and services. Its drawbacks (each is a "money solves this" item):
1. **Double coincidence of wants** — both parties must want what the other sells
2. **Indivisibility** — one agent's single indivisible unit may be worth more than what they're trying to buy
3. Agents may **not wish to exchange all** their goods for other goods
4. **No common measure of value** — oranges priced in pears, pears in bread; multiple transactions become unmanageable

### The definition and the five qualities 🔴
**Money** = any **generally accepted medium of exchange**. A **medium of exchange** = any asset that can be used to **purchase goods and services or to repay debts**. (The wheat farmer no longer needs a wine producer who wants wheat.)

For money to work as a medium of exchange it must be:
| # | Quality |
|---|---|
| i | **Readily acceptable** |
| ii | Have a **known value** |
| iii | **Easily divisible** |
| iv | **High value relative to its weight** |
| v | **Difficult to counterfeit** |

Why **precious metals (gold, silver)** served for so long (into the early 20th century): known value, divisible, high value-to-weight, hard to counterfeit — all five. **Memory hook: A-K-D-W-C** (acceptable, known, divisible, weight, counterfeit).

### The three functions 🔴 — the exam's standard list
| Function | What it means | Precious-metal example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Medium of exchange** | Used to buy goods/services or settle debts | Gold coins change hands |
| **2. Store of wealth (value)** | Holds value over time | Gold doesn't perish and has high value-to-bulk — **BUT** this depends on the **belief that others will continue to value it** (demand for ornaments, jewellery). If gold became less valued year after year, it would fail as a store of wealth **and might lose its medium-of-exchange status too** |
| **3. Unit of account / measure of value** | A single, unitary **measure of value** for all goods and services | All prices, debts, and wealth recorded in gold/silver coin value |

⚠️ **The belief dependency is testable:** a store-of-wealth function rests on **continued demand/belief**, not just physical durability. And the functions are linked — losing one (store of wealth) can cost another (medium of exchange).

### From gold to paper — the promissory note 🟡
- Carrying gold was unsafe → individuals left gold with **goldsmiths**, who issued **receipts** → receipts traded directly for goods → each receipt was a **promise to pay gold on demand** → paper money as a **proxy for a physical commodity**.
- Goldsmiths evolved into **banks**, issuing **promissory notes** used in commerce.
- The key realization: **not all gold would be withdrawn at once** — most sat in the vault while ownership changed hands → the idle fraction could be **lent at interest** → **the early banks created money**.

### Fractional reserve banking and money creation 🔴 THE CALCULATION
- **Money creation**'s potency depends on how much banks keep **in reserve** to meet withdrawals.
- **Fractional reserve banking** = lending customers' money to others on the assumption that **not all customers want all their money back at once**.
- **Reserve requirement** = the fraction of deposits banks retain (10% in the example). Set by banks themselves in the simple model; in some economies **set by the central bank** → a potential tool for affecting money growth.

**Exhibit 1 — the chain (reserve requirement 10%):**
| Bank | Deposit (liability) | Reserves (asset) | Loan (asset) |
|---|---|---|---|
| First Bank of Nations | €100 | €10 | €90 |
| Second Bank of Nations | €90 | €9 | €81 |
| Third Bank of Nations | €81 | €8.1 | €72.9 |
| ... continues until nothing is left to lend | | | |

Each balance sheet balances (assets = liabilities). The borrower spends the loan; the seller deposits it in the next bank; that bank keeps 10% and lends 90%. **Money is created when a bank makes a loan.**

```
Total money created  =  New deposit / Reserve requirement            (Equation 1)
                     =  €100 / 0.10  =  €1,000

Money multiplier     =  1 / Reserve requirement  =  1 / 0.10  =  10
```
(Verified: the deposit series 100 + 90 + 81 + 72.9 + ... sums to 1,000.)

- The €1,000 is the **sum of all deposits** now in the banking system. The original €100 was the **catalyst for €1,000 of transactions** — not the whole of economic growth, but "an important component in economic activity."
- 🔴 **Direction:** **smaller reserve requirement → larger multiplier → more money created.** (1/0.05 = 20; 1/0.20 = 5.)

⚠️ **Three calculation traps:**
1. **"Money created" = Equation 1's TOTAL, including the original deposit.** ✓ Example 2 Q2 confirmed the convention: *"how much money could be created with a £100 deposit at 5%?"* → **£2,000** (= 100/0.05), **not £1,900**. The curriculum treats the whole deposit chain as "created"; the deposit-minus-original figure (£1,900) is the **distractor**. Only subtract the original if the stem explicitly says "*additional* to the initial deposit" or "*new* loans."
2. **Multiplier vs. total:** 1/r is the multiplier (10); deposit × multiplier is the total (1,000). Don't report one for the other.
3. **The reserve requirement is a fraction** — 10% enters as 0.10, not 10.

**Example 2 results (3/3 ✓):**
- Q1: medium-of-exchange qualities → *easily divisible AND good store of value* — the "and" was correct because the curriculum lists both; distractors each broke one quality (*low* value-to-weight; "conservative investment" isn't a quality).
- Q2: 5% reserve, £100 → **£2,000** (see trap 1).
- Q3: functions → all three (measure of value, medium of exchange, store of wealth); the "only" options are the distractors.

**How the central bank plugs in (preview of the policy section):** the central bank can **buy €100 of government securities**, crediting the seller's bank account → that €100 deposit starts the same chain → money supply expands. It can also **lend reserves directly to banks**, creating **excess reserves** (above the requirement) that support new loans. (🔗 M3 §11: these are the OMO and reserve tools, now with the multiplier attached.) Prudent banks hold reserves sufficient for **stressful credit conditions**, which can mean more than the requirement.

### Definitions of money — narrow vs. broad 🔴
- Simple world (coins only): money = total gold/silver coins in circulation.
- With fractional reserve banking: money = **notes and coins in circulation PLUS all bank deposits**.
- Harder cases: cheques and **debit cards** draw on deposits (money); **savings/time deposits** are easily transferred to current accounts (arguably money); **credit cards are NOT money** — a credit-card payment is a **deferred payment**, not a transfer of a money balance. ⚠️ Standard trap.
- **The more complex the financial system, the harder money is to define.**

| Measure | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Narrow money** | Notes and coins in circulation **+ other very highly liquid deposits** |
| **Broad money** | Narrow money **+ the entire range of liquid assets that can be used to make purchases** |

- Most central banks publish **both**, plus intermediate measures. Definitions **vary across economies** → **international comparisons are difficult** (same caveat as unemployment measurement, 🔗 M4 §4).

## 2. Money: Quantity Theory, Supply and Demand, Fisher Effect (§5.03)
**Exam importance: 🔴 VERY HIGH — MV = PY and the Fisher equation are both guaranteed formula items; the three money-demand motives and the money-market diagram are reliable concept items. This section is also the module's first polarity-dense stretch (interest rate ↔ bond price ↔ money demand), so the direction discipline from M3–M4 applies.**

### The quantity theory of money 🔴 FORMULA
**Quantity theory** asserts that **total spending (in money terms) is proportional to the quantity of money.** Expressed through the **quantity equation of exchange**:

```
M × V  =  P × Y                                       (Equation 2)

M = quantity of money
V = velocity of circulation (avg. number of times a unit of currency changes hands per period)
P = average price level
Y = real output
```

- **It is an accounting identity:** money used to buy all goods and services (M × V) = the monetary value of that output (P × Y). 🔗 M4 §5: velocity = nominal GDP / M is just this equation rearranged (V = PY/M).
- **The theory's assumption:** **V is approximately constant** → P × Y (nominal spending) is **proportional to M**.

**Worked example (verified):** M = 500, V = 4 → nominal spending PY = 2,000. If real output Y = 1,000 → P = 2. If M rises 10% with V and Y unchanged → P rises 10%.

### Money neutrality 🔴
If **money neutrality** holds, an increase in M does **not** affect **Y** (real output) or **V** (if real output is unaffected, there's no need for money to change hands faster) → the entire effect falls on **P**: **the price level rises.**
```
M ↑ , V̄ , Ȳ   →   P ↑   (proportionally)
```
🔗 M3 §11: this is the formal statement behind "money expansion raises output in the short run but only prices in the long run."

### Monetarists and the causality question 🔴
- Quantity theory → the simple idea that **the price level / inflation rate can be controlled by controlling money-supply growth**. Economists holding this view = **Monetarists**: causality runs **from money growth → inflation**. (🔗 M4 §5 — Friedman's "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon.") Some governments have applied this logic to fight inflation.
- ⚠️ **The curriculum's caveat (testable):** causality may run **the other way** — from **real activity → money supply** — meaning the quantity of money in circulation is **determined by the level of economic activity**, not vice versa. An option stating that money growth *necessarily* causes inflation overstates the curriculum's position.

### The demand for money — three motives 🔴
**Demand for money** = the amount of wealth citizens choose to hold **as money** rather than bonds or equities.

| Motive | Balances held for | Rises with | Falls with |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transactions** | Financing transactions | Average value of transactions → grows with **GDP**; the **ratio of transactions balances to GDP stays fairly stable** | — |
| **Precautionary** | A **buffer against unforeseen events** needing money | Volume and value of transactions → **GDP** (a $100 buffer is small for a firm transacting millions) | — |
| **Speculative** | Holding money **in anticipation that other assets will decline in value** | **Perceived risk** of other financial assets ↑ | **Expected return** on other assets ↑ (holding money forgoes the bond return) |

### The money market — Exhibit 2 🔴
The "price" of money = the **nominal interest rate** that could be earned by lending it.

```
Nominal i
   |  MD (downward sloping)        MS (vertical)
I₁ |········\  ← excess SUPPLY of money (M₀ − M₁) → agents buy bonds
   |         \      → bond prices ↑, interest rate ↓ toward I₀
I₀ |----------●----------  equilibrium: no excess money balances
   |           \
I₂ |············\·· ← excess DEMAND for money (M₂ − M₀) → agents sell bonds
   |             \     → bond prices ↓, interest rate ↑ toward I₀
   +---M₁---M₀---M₂------- Quantity of nominal money
```

| Curve | Shape | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **MS** (money supply) | **Vertical** | Assumes a **fixed nominal amount of money** circulating at any one time |
| **MD** (money demand) | **Downward sloping** | As interest rates rise, the **speculative demand for money falls** |

**The adjustment mechanism — trace both directions:**
- Rate at **I₁ > I₀** → excess **supply** of money (M₀ − M₁) → agents **buy bonds** with excess balances → bond **prices ↑** → **rates ↓** to I₀.
- Rate at **I₂ < I₀** → excess **demand** for money (M₂ − M₀) → agents **sell bonds** to raise money → bond **prices ↓** → **rates ↑** to I₀.
- **I₀** = the rate at which **no excess money balances exist**. The curriculum notes the fixed-supply assumption may not hold in practice, but the adjustment dynamics "essentially still hold."

⚠️ **Bond price ↔ interest rate is inverse** — every chain above passes through it. (Fixed Income will formalize this; here it's the transmission step.)

**Central bank raises the money supply (M₀ → M₂):** MS shifts **right** → money is more plentiful, hence **less valuable** → its price, the **interest rate, falls** — and the **price level rises** (the Exhibit 2 restatement of quantity theory).

Neutrality is a **long-run** proposition (official Q4 ✓); short-run non-neutrality is what gives the central bank its lever. 🔗 M3 §11: money expansion raises output in the short run, only prices in the long run — the same split.

### Why it's "more complex in practice" — the real-resources argument 🟡
- The initial money increase creates **excess cash**; people lend it (buying bonds), deposit it, or **spend it on goods and services**.
- But the economy's **capacity to produce** depends on **real things** — natural resources, capital, labour (factors supplied by households). **More money does not change the availability of real things.**
- → Some economists conclude the **long-run impact of an exogenous money-supply increase is a rise in the aggregate price level** — neutrality by another route. (🔗 M3 §10: the LRAS logic.)

### The Fisher effect 🔴 FORMULA
Named for **Irving Fisher**; **directly related to money neutrality**. The **real rate of interest is stable over time**, so **changes in nominal rates are the result of changes in expected inflation.**

```
R_nom  =  R_real  +  πᵉ                                (Equation 3)

R_nom  = nominal interest rate
R_real = required real rate of interest
πᵉ     = expected inflation over the horizon
```

**Worked (verified):** real 2% + expected inflation 3% → nominal 5%. Nominal 6.5% with real 2.5% → expected inflation 4%.

- **Neutrality implication:** over the long term, the money supply / its growth rate **does not affect R_real** but **does affect inflation and inflation expectations** → and hence R_nom.

## 3. The Costs of Inflation (§5.04)
**Exam importance: 🔴 HIGH — "unexpected inflation is the costly kind" and the borrower/lender transfer are near-guaranteed; the TV-manufacturer story is the curriculum's mechanism for "inflation uncertainty exacerbates the cycle." Short section, dense with direction calls.**

### Expected vs. unexpected — the distinction the whole section rests on 🔴
| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Expected inflation** | The level of inflation economic agents **expect** in the future |
| **Unexpected inflation** | Inflation **above or below** what was expected — the **surprise** component |

Context: the inflation of the **1970s** made inflation control a policy priority. The question "why is inflation bad?" turns on this split.

### The thought experiment — three worlds 🟡
| World | Do agents care about inflation? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1. All prices (incl. asset prices) perfectly indexed** | **No** — indifferent | Prices +10% → salaries and everything else +10% → nothing real changes |
| **2. Not indexed, but inflation high and perfectly predictable** | A little — must think about it, but not hard | Everyone bargains for +10% wages, firms plan +10% prices → the expectation is a **self-fulfilling prophecy** |
| **3. Not indexed AND high and unpredictable** | **Yes — this is the costly world** | The 1970s–80s in the US, France, UK, Italy, Canada |

**Takeaway:** the cost of inflation comes from it being **unindexed and unpredictable**, not from the level alone.

### Why UNEXPECTED inflation is the costly kind 🔴
**Fully anticipated inflation** can be **factored into wage negotiations and priced into business and financial contracts** — so it does little harm. Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth:

| Outcome | Who wins | Who loses | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation **higher** than anticipated | **Borrowers** | Lenders | Real value of the debt **declines** |
| Inflation **lower** than anticipated | **Lenders** | Borrowers | Real value of the debt payment **rises** |

⚠️ **Polarity drill:** debt is fixed in nominal terms (🔗 M4 §5 deflation mechanism, same logic). Surprise inflation *erodes* what the borrower repays in real terms → borrower gains. Build the chain from "nominal debt is fixed," never from intuition about who "suffers" from inflation.

**Second cost — the uncertainty premium:** if inflation is very **uncertain or volatile**, lenders demand a **premium** to compensate → **borrowing costs rise** → can **discourage investment** and reduce economic activity. (🔗 §2 Fisher: this is the *risk premium* component of the nominal rate, Example 4 Q2.)

### Inflation uncertainty can exacerbate the cycle — the TV manufacturer 🔴
**Inflation uncertainty** = the degree to which agents view future inflation as **hard to forecast**.

```
TV prices observed +10%
  → manufacturer infers: demand ↑ (or supply ↓) for TVs specifically
  → extends factory, hires workers, produces more
  → tries to sell the extra TVs → NO extra demand exists
  → the +10% was a GENERAL +10% in all consumer prices, not a relative-price signal
  → surplus stock, surplus capacity, too many workers
  → cuts production, lays off, shelves investment for a long time
```

- **The lesson:** unanticipated inflation **reduces the information content of market prices** — agents can't tell a *relative* price change (a real signal) from a *general* price change (noise).
- Scaled up, unanticipated rises or falls in the general price level can **exacerbate — and in extreme cases cause — booms and busts**. (🔗 M1 §2: a price change is only a demand signal if it's *relative*; inflation destroys that reading.)

### The policy conclusion 🔴
The consensus of the last two to three decades: **unanticipated and high inflation affects real things — employment, investment, profits** — therefore **controlling inflation should be one of the main goals of macroeconomic policy.** (This is the justification for the central bank objectives in the next section.)

> **One-liner to carry:** *anticipated inflation is priced in; unanticipated inflation redistributes wealth, raises borrowing costs via a premium, and corrupts price signals.*

Definition traps: a TOOL (money targets), a USE (redistribution), an
  OBJECTIVE (balanced budget), or the wrong ACTOR → all distractors
Governments matter: large employer/spender + largest borrower

── MONEY (§5.02) ──
Barter problems: double coincidence | indivisibility | not all goods
  tradable | no common measure of value
Money = generally accepted MEDIUM OF EXCHANGE
Five qualities (A-K-D-W-C): acceptable | known value | divisible |
  high value-to-WEIGHT | hard to counterfeit
Three functions: medium of exchange | store of wealth (needs BELIEF
  others will value it) | unit of account / measure of value
Goldsmiths → receipts → promissory notes → banks lend idle gold → money
Fractional reserve: lend on assumption not all withdraw at once
  Money created = deposit / reserve req   (€100 / 0.10 = €1,000 TOTAL)
  Money multiplier = 1 / reserve req      (smaller r → bigger multiplier) ✓
  ⭐ "money created" = the TOTAL incl. original (£100 @ 5% → £2,000);
     deposit-minus-original is the distractor
Narrow = notes/coins + very liquid deposits | Broad = narrow + all
  liquid assets usable for purchases; CREDIT CARDS ≠ money (deferred)

── QUANTITY THEORY & FISHER (§5.03) ──
M × V = P × Y  (identity; theory assumes V ≈ constant)
Neutrality: M ↑ → Y, V unchanged → P ↑ proportionally — LONG RUN ✓
  (conditional: P ↑ "as long as V doesn't fall enough / Y unchanged")
  ⭐ Monetary policy ASSUMES money is NOT neutral in the SHORT RUN
Monetarists: money growth → inflation ✓; curriculum caveat: causality
  may run activity → money
MONEY DEMAND (three pairs ⭐ missed twice): transactions → PURCHASES (∝ GDP)
  | precautionary → UNFORESEEN buffer (∝ GDP)
  | speculative → OTHER ASSETS: ↓ as their expected return ↑,
    ↑ as their perceived risk ↑
MONEY MARKET: MS vertical (fixed nominal M); MD down (speculative ↓ as i ↑)
  price of money = nominal interest rate; I₀ = no excess balances
  i > I₀ (I₁): excess money SUPPLY → BUY bonds → bond P ↑ → i ↓ ✓
  i < I₀ (I₂): excess demand → SELL bonds → bond P ↓ → i ↑
  ⭐ M ↑ → MS right → i ↓ (and P ↑); equilibrium money HELD rises
Real-resources: more money ≠ more labour/capital → LR effect on P only
FISHER: R_nom = R_real + πᵉ  (EXPECTED inflation; real rate is stable) ✓
  ⭐ "real rate is stable" is FISHER's proposition (not neutrality)
  full version adds a RISK PREMIUM for uncertainty
  TIPS breakeven = nominal − linked yield ≈ πᵉ
OWNERSHIP MAP ⭐: stable real rate → Fisher | money → only P in LR →
  neutrality | MV = PY, spending ∝ M → quantity theory | control
  inflation via money growth → Monetarists

── COSTS OF INFLATION (§5.04) ──
Expected = anticipated level | Unexpected = the SURPRISE (above or below)
Indexed world → indifferent; predictable → self-fulfilling; high AND
  unpredictable → costly (1970s–80s)
UNEXPECTED is costly: anticipated inflation is priced into wages/contracts
  Inflation > expected → BORROWERS win (real debt ↓)
  Inflation < expected → LENDERS win (real payment ↑)
  Volatile inflation → lenders demand premium → borrowing cost ↑ → I ↓
TV manufacturer: general +10% read as relative signal → overexpand →
  bust. Unanticipated inflation REDUCES INFORMATION CONTENT OF PRICES
  → can exacerbate or cause booms/busts
Consensus: unanticipated/high inflation hits employment, investment,
  profits → controlling inflation = main macro goal
Central banks worry most about UNANTICIPATED (moderate) inflation;
  very low anticipated inflation costs more than moderate ✓
